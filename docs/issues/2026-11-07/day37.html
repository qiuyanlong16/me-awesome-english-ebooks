It is probably intubated to help breathing; another tube trickles milk directly to the stomach. If you put tape on this fragile being, you will tear its skin. But if you leave it, it will cry. Clémentine Goldszal, a French journalist, is consumed by this image and by a single imperative: to find out what birth is like for newborns, and how they experience the multiple medical interventions that may, in a worst case, immediately follow. She would interview them if she could. But of course there is no communication. When her own son Lucien was born, what she

took as sweet kittenish sounds turned out to be signs of acute respiratory distress. An apparently lively baby may, in fact, be having convulsions. She decided to “construct a phenomenology of the newborn” by spending many months as an observer in the neonatal unit of the Necker in Paris. This is a superb hospital, especially for its care of premature babies, rated seventh in the world. Sick babies are rushed there from all over France. Standing in a corner of the incubator rooms, out of the way, she sees nurses ceaselessly watching the beeping monitors, checking on breathing and feeding. In other rooms she watches operations so delicate, performed with miniature instruments, that a margin of error of a millimetre would be a catastrophe. What she wanted to decipher was the attitude of medical staff to the newborn: whether or not they considered them fully human, individual and even having personalities. She had viewed Lucien as merely “a generic entity” for some weeks, and found it hard to say his name. But at Necker newborn patients were all called by their names and treated tenderly. They were assumed to have agency, if only to close their eyes or poo in their nappies. When those nappies were changed, blankets were put over the incubators to shield their privacy. After all, in French law, they were individuals as soon as they drew breath and no longer occupied their mother’s womb. And yet without their consent, these babies were invaded. They were injected, intubated, placed among noisy machines and blinded by harsh lights. Tiny catheters were sent in to deliver fluids. In France 94% of babies born between 27 and 31 weeks left the hospital in perfect health. (At 25 weeks, though, the figure fell to 60%.)
