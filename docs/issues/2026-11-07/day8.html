Asia | The next big one

Asia

Sitting at the junction of four tectonic plates (see map), Japan suffers around a fifth of the world’s earthquakes of magnitude 6 or greater. The earthquake and tsunami in 2011 in Tohoku in the north-east of the main island of Honshu, which triggered a nuclear meltdown, are strong memories. In 2024 the Noto peninsula was devastated by a magnitude 7.6 earthquake, and another powerful one struck Kumamoto this summer. Torrential rains and floods, intensified by climate change, pose a growing threat, too. Yet even these calamities may be dwarfed by what lies ahead, says Kawata Yoshiaki, a disaster-management expert at Kansai University who advised the government about the agency. Seismologists warn of two catastrophic earthquakes; their statistical model suggests a 70% chance of a magnitude 7 or higher quake directly hitting the Tokyo metropolitan area in the next 30 years. Over the same period another feared megaquake along the Nankai Trough off Japan’s Pacific coast has a 60-90% probability. In the worst case, such a quake could kill nearly 300,000 people, force 12.3m to evacuate and cause ¥292trn ($1.8trn) in economic losses, equivalent to more than 40% of Japan’s annual GDP. Mr Kawata expects long blackouts and broken supply chains that would leave vast numbers short of food. Japan is already a global leader in disaster preparedness, especially in hardware such as earthquake-resistant buildings and early-warning

systems. With the new agency, the government hopes to streamline its software and put more resources towards preparing for the bigger and more complex disasters that may lie ahead. Until now, responsibility for disaster management has been distributed across different ministries and levels of government. The prime minister oversaw such efforts with support from a small unit in the Cabinet Office, staffed by officials seconded from other ministries, who rotate out every few years. Ueshima Yasuhiro of Japan Platform, a network of aid groups, likens the government’s previous approach to “putting on a Band-Aid” whenever disaster strikes, rather than treating underlying risks. The new agency, with around 350 staff, aims to boost preparedness by building up institutional knowledge about dealing with a range of potential disasters. Creating a single centralised body should help address one of the Japanese system’s biggest weaknesses: a notoriously siloed bureaucracy that struggles to co-ordinate across institutions. Consider hospitals, says Mr Kawata, pointing out that they could lose power during a big Tokyo earthquake, putting many thousands of patients at risk; planning evacuations would involve the health ministry. The fisheries agency, meanwhile, could help fishing communities protect themselves against tsunamis. While Mr Hosono will handle day-to-day operations, Takaichi Sanae, the prime minister, will officially head the agency, which will also have powers to make suggestions to other ministries. Climate change and more extreme weather are demanding vigilance towards less familiar threats. Tokyo experienced 37 consecutive days of rain until the beginning of October this year, the longest stretch since records began in 1886. In nearby Chiba, record rains wreaked havoc in August, followed by a flood weeks later. Waki Yuichiro, a 52-year-old resident, whose house was flooded and car ruined, says he had prepared for earthquakes, “but I never imagined flooding would affect us…In the future, we have to prepare for the unexpected—anything can happen.” Tanaka Yasuo, a volunteer who visits Chiba to repair air-conditioners damaged by the flooding—essential appliances during Japan’s sweltering summer—says that in the past he used to have time to rest between disasters; now they come so frequently that “I can barely take a break”.

Japan’s demography, with its large number of old people, makes it vulnerable in other ways. One growing concern is saigai kanrenshi, or disaster-related deaths: people who survive the initial catastrophe but later die from prolonged stress or deteriorating health during evacuation. After an earthquake in Kumamoto in 2016, such deaths accounted for more than 80% of the toll. In Noto, too, they made up 70% of deaths. Poor conditions in evacuation centres, where survivors still often sleep on crowded gymnasium floors and without adequate sanitation or nutritious food, can make things worse. Such evacuation centres have hardly improved over the years. Ishiba Shigeru, a former prime minister who championed the disaster agency, blames a culture that prizes perseverance and “idealises disaster survivors who endure hardship”. Sugano Taku of Osaka Metropolitan University points out that municipalities with inadequate resources are being left to handle them on their own; the new agency is supposed to offer them guidance and support. But it is just one part of Japan’s approach to disasters, which rests on three principles: kojo, government assistance; kyojo, mutual help; and jijo, self- help. In Kumamoto, Fujino Tatsuo and other volunteers clambered onto damaged roofs, removing heavy kawara, traditional Japanese tiles that could fall in another earthquake. Nearby, Miyabara Yukimi’s home had collapsed, though her whole family miraculously crawled out of the rubble. She and her husband spent weeks sleeping in a lorry before volunteers helped them build a makeshift shelter. “It feels like we lost everything—but we still have to make the first step,” says Ms Miyabara. On its first day the new disaster agency kicked off by issuing an “emergency request” urging local governments to strengthen disaster preparations and citizens to take precautions themselves. Mr Hosono stressed that jijo remained essential. “We cannot achieve this by relying on the government alone,” he said of his ambition to make Japan even more of an example to the rest of the world in disaster preparedness. Dead Starlink terminals in Myanmar are getting people killed Has Elon Musk taken another war zone offline? Nov 5th 2026 UNDER BRITISH rule, Burma boasted the largest tungsten mine in the world at Maw Chi, near the border with Thailand. Decades of war and misrule have slashed the mine’s output. But labourers working in small teams at Maw Chi still dig up the metal before sending it on to Thailand and global markets to make aeroplanes, missiles and semiconductors. That has made Maw Chi a prize in the war between the military junta which seized power in Myanmar in 2021 and rebels opposing it. Two years ago rebels captured Maw Chi and started to put its revenue stream towards their war effort. The junta responded with occasional air strikes on the mine. Rebels countered by creating an ingenious early- warning system using Starlink, the space-based internet service that is run by

SpaceX. When the junta launched an attack aircraft, a spotter near the airfield would alert civilians at the mine site to take shelter. But on September 1st that warning system failed. A jet bombed the mine at Maw Chi without any alert, says a local rebel commander, killing five and injuring six. One of the Starlink terminals used for the early-warning system had been cut off, leaving the miners in the dark. Starlink has become essential in parts of Myanmar riven by conflict or governed by rebels. The junta has turned off cellular services in many of these areas, leaving rebel armies and humanitarian organisations reliant on connections to SpaceX’s satellites in low-earth orbit. The democratic government-in-exile uses Starlink to remotely run schools and hospitals in liberated areas, where too large a physical footprint would attract attention from the junta’s air force. In these places Starlink is ubiquitous even though, on paper, the service is not available in Myanmar. Terminals are procured elsewhere and brought into the country illegally. Until recently, SpaceX has tended to turn a benevolent blind eye towards their use. The episode at Maw Chi was not an isolated incident. Rebels, aid workers and teachers say that some Starlink terminals across Myanmar have been shut down by SpaceX since July. Many others are still up and running, they say, and it is not clear how the company has decided which to cut off.

SpaceX has not offered any public explanation, but it has told some aid workers in Myanmar that, under pressure from the American government, it is trying to cut off scam centres based in the country that rely on its services to carry out their transnational frauds. A State Department spokesman says that it is working with private firms and their financiers to combat scam centres, while “preserving access” for those using the technology for “legitimate purposes”. Rumours of a “whitelist” maintained by the State Department have been swirling among aid groups, but no one seems to know what they need to do to be added to it. For reasons that remain murky, SpaceX had recently throttled Starlink connections in Ukraine, reportedly after talks between Elon Musk, its founder, and President Donald Trump. Its motives in Myanmar are also obscure. Analysts have seen no signs of drones using Starlink in Myanmar, where rebels are on shoestring budgets. New terminals smuggled into Myanmar could cost as much as $8,500 each, say aid workers. That is far too pricey for the poorly funded rebels to simply strap one to a drone made to explode on impact. But the terminals have been invaluable in letting democratic forces offer services in areas they control. Hospitals under the National Unity Government (NUG), the pro-democracy government-in-exile, use it to consult specialists abroad. And students across Myanmar use it to attend NUG schools remotely. As terminals go dead, students are dropping out. The head teacher at an NUG school says that of 270 students who started the year there, 40 have stopped attending. Perhaps the most vital use of Starlink has been in early-warning systems like the one at Maw Chi. In a curious twist, some of these systems, say former American officials, were secretly set up by USAID in 2024. Even before Mr Musk put the aid agency through the wood-chipper last year, SpaceX had doubts about this. “They had lots of questions about the legal implications of allowing their terminals to be used in Myanmar,” says a former American official who was involved. In particular, they say, SpaceX was wary of letting its terminals be used for lethal purposes. Shutting them down is also proving lethal. ■
