The fight for the soul of the Australian right Can a special-forces veteran stop the rise of Pauline Hanson? Nov 5th 2026 Senator pauline hanson swaggered into Secret Harbour on October 30th like a general inspecting troops in newly conquered territory. It was her first visit since One Nation, her ragtag populist party, won a by-election in the coastal town. The race for a seat in the Western Australia state parliament was the latest in an “orange wave” of wins for One Nation in elections this year. But Secret Harbour is special: the town sits inside the federal constituency of one of Ms Hanson’s most formidable opponents, the special-forces veteran and Liberal Party frontbencher Andrew Hastie. Once dismissed as a gadfly, Ms Hanson is now a real force. In January One Nation overtook the Liberals in the polls and still has a lead. It is neck-and- neck with the governing Labor Party. It still lacks much of the scaffolding of a national organisation, but Ms Hanson has shown that she can turn voter

anger at the major parties into seats. So her party has a real shot at replacing the Liberals as the official opposition at the next election in 2028, and an outside one at forming the next government. The election in February of Angus Taylor as the Liberals’ new leader has not boosted the party’s fortunes. Mr Taylor, attacked by Ms Hanson as a “smarmy management consultant”, has mostly avoided confrontation with her. But Mr Hastie has taken her on directly. Some Liberals argue that, against her, he would be a stronger leader than Mr Taylor. “It was obvious that the better choice would have been Hastie,” says Malcolm Turnbull, a former prime minister and Liberal leader. “I’m sure he’ll lead the Liberal Party some day—if he doesn’t lose his seat.” That Mr Hastie might face two such different futures is largely a consequence of one decision that he made: to testify against a fellow soldier accused of war crimes. That has led to an emotional debate on the right, one which Ms Hanson has been eager to use to her advantage. “I was subpoenaed, and then under oath required to tell the truth,” Mr Hastie tells The Economist. “Would you rather me be in contempt of court and lie?” Wildly divergent answers to that question have this year been tearing the Australian right apart. Mr Hastie is now under attack from Ms Hanson and

her powerful allies like Gina Rinehart, a mining magnate, and Kerry Stokes, a media baron, some of them once supporters of the Liberals. In July Ms Hanson published a video calling Mr Hastie a “traitor” and encouraging Liberals to abandon him. The strategy seems to be working. Tony Abbott, the president of the Liberal party and another former prime minister, has distanced himself from Mr Hastie. They are trying, he says, “to destroy me personally”. But the stakes for the Australian right are far higher than that. The veteran of the elite Special Air Service regiment is an unlikely hate figure for the populist right. Mr Hastie has led a push over the past year to drag the Liberal Party to the right on issues that animate populists. His call for lower net migration is now party policy. He is against women in combat. Like many populists, he bemoans the demise of Australian manufacturing, arguing for a more active industrial policy. But he parts ways with them when it comes to the rule of law. Mr Hastie links his stand against war crimes to his Christianity. He testified against a fellow soldier, he tells supporters, under an oath before God. He is more open about his faith than most Australian politicians: “I learned more about politics and leadership watching my father [a Presbyterian minister] at church than anywhere else.” But “I am not a Christian nationalist.” By contrast, Ms Hanson has called Australia a Christian country, yet dodged questions about her own beliefs. The lessons from his father’s church sometimes point in unexpected directions. Mr Hastie opposes abortion and gay marriage, but rejects Ms Hanson’s call for a “monocultural” Australia. Worse yet, in his view, are her fans’ calls for racial criteria for immigrants. He recalls his father’s church in Ashfield, then one of Sydney’s most diverse suburbs, which held services in several Asian languages because “everyone is made in God’s image”. Immigrants, he says, should be chosen for their values and skills rather than their ethnicity. That same philosophy has allowed Mr Hastie to make friends across the aisle. Take Senator Penny Wong, the foreign minister and a Labor stalwart. Ms Wong, gay and born in Malaysia, might seem an unlikely partner. But the two worked well together when Mr Hastie was a backbencher and Ms Wong was in opposition. “It’s always been clear to me…that he is one of the

few on his side who believes there is a national interest above partisanship,” says Ms Wong. “While Andrew isn’t really where modern Australia is, he understands that you can’t out-Pauline Pauline.” That hints at a potential way through for Mr Hastie. Once known as the Liberal Party’s rising right-winger, his fight with One Nation is broadening his appeal. He has no plans to give up his conservative bona fides, but he is seeking out new allies. Within his own party, once-critical moderate grandees like Mr Turnbull now offer cautious praise. At a fundraiser last month 80% of participants were new donors. And he has become a more frequent guest on public television and radio shows, whose viewers tend to skew centre-left. That could leave him in a stronger position to lead the Liberal Party in the future. But first he must see off Ms Hanson’s campaign to brand him among the conservative base as a traitor. He still looks to be under siege. The home- affairs minister has cited unspecified threats in advising police to upgrade security at his home and office. “It’s a bit unnerving,” Mr Hastie says. “But I’m a Christian. I pray about these things, and I’m at peace about it.” ■ For exclusive coverage of Asian politics, economics and security, sign up to Asia Bulletin, our weekly subscriber-only newsletter. Cheap data is great for Indians but not for India’s reputation The internet brings out the worst in everyone Nov 5th 2026 “BITCH LASAGNA” is the name of a song by PewDiePie. It is also a two- word social history of the relationship between India and the internet. For five years Mr Pie, a Swede whose real name is irrelevant, was the owner- operator of YouTube’s most popular channel. In early 2019, after several months of back-and-forth, he was decisively overtaken by T-Series, a Bollywood studio. In the run-up to his defeat the Swedish streamer posted what his fans described as a “diss”, or insult, track and what anyone else would consider to be bordering on bigotry. Its lyrics include references to open defecation, ridicule of Indian languages and a meme about “bobs or vegana” (don’t ask). The song’s title alludes to a Facebook Messenger screenshot of an Indian

man requesting unclothed self-portraits from a woman and, on being ignored, hurling the presumably auto-corrected epithet at her. The internet really does bring out the worst in everyone. Mr Pie’s battle with T-Series marked a hinge point in India’s internet adoption. In 2016 Mukesh Ambani, an industrialist who inherited a petrochemical company, took literally the maxim that “data is the new oil” and launched Jio, a mobile network with rock-bottom prices and generous data allowances. Almost overnight the share of India’s population covered by a modern mobile-broadband network jumped from 4% to 74%. A price war followed. Internet users doubled in just three years from a sixth of India’s people when Jio launched to a third when T-Series triumphed, rising to over two-thirds today. A billion Indians use the internet, or three for every American. Not satisfied with bringing Indians online, Jio, which in October went public in one of the country’s largest listings, also gives them something to do once they get there. Its streaming service, Hotstar, has more than 450m monthly users, well ahead of Netflix or Amazon’s Prime Video. But neither Jio nor anyone else has managed to build a social space Indians prefer to Western ones. Indians are the largest nationality on YouTube, Instagram and Facebook and a significant minority on X, Reddit and Snapchat. Their influx onto these platforms has not been great for India’s image in the world. “Bitch Lasagna”, though tasteless, reflects a common stereotype online of Indian men as forever propositioning women on social media. The idea of India as an unsanitary place, too, is depressingly common. And Indians have of late acquired a reputation as fierce nationalists with thin skins and foul mouths. As one viral post on X put it, “Never invade Russia in winter. Never attack India by Twitter.” Some of the criticism is overblown. Crude men and noisy nationalists exist everywhere, but the sheer scale of the Indian contingent makes them more noticeable. Some of it is the result of India’s openness. Because Indians use the same platforms and often post in English, the country’s flaws are more visible than those of other countries.
