Finance & economics | Free Exchange William Nordhaus, favourite economist of the right and the left The Nobel-prizewinner was fascinated by what happens when markets fail Nov 5th 2026 WILLIAM NORDHAUS had faith in the tools of his profession. For decades he helped run Yale University’s economics department, where he arrived in 1967, aged 26, and stayed until he died on October 30th. He edited “Economics”, a textbook still used by American undergraduates. And he was convinced that economic methods could be useful to other disciplines, leaving wonkish topics behind in pursuit of debates that consumed policymakers: the climate, technology and taxes. His faith made him unflinching—and unafraid of a fight. No other economist of his generation worked across such a breadth of controversial topics. He pioneered carbon prices (which helped earn him a Nobel prize in 2018), pleasing America’s progressives and irking its conservatives. He also

defended billionaires, with the opposite effect. As a result, he became a rare economist to win the respect of the left and right alike. Economics relies on the price system. Mr Nordhaus spent his life calculating prices that are missing. His doctoral thesis examined such failures in the knowledge economy, and suggested the optimal patent life for inventions that minimises underinvestment in copyable ideas and maximises social welfare. In the 1970s he decided that building theoretical models of technological change was “too hard” (and left the task to Paul Romer, with whom he shared the Nobel). So he turned to climate change instead. Although the practice of climate economics would in time become politically fraught, the theory was manageable. Economic growth depended on energy. Firms that produced and used energy were not bearing the cost of the resulting carbon pollution. The warming that would ensue could hurt growth. What to do? One research group at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology favoured abandoning growth altogether. Mr Nordhaus disagreed. The problem, he thought, was a lack of a model that brought together the scientific and economic equations to connect growth, emissions and the environment. Mr Nordhaus spent the next two decades trying to construct one. In 1992 he published a paper outlining the “dynamic integrated climate economy” (DICE) model. Its more than 20 equations eventually ranged from basic economic ones he taught undergraduates to scientific predictions about the risk of catastrophes and forecasts about how humans would adapt to a hotter world. Throw in a discount rate, the value placed on the lives of future generations relative to the present, and the model churns out the path of emissions reductions that would not plunge millions into poverty today, as a result of insufficient energy production, or tomorrow, if global warming wrecked the economy. The carbon price which would set the world on that path equalises the cost of abating an extra tonne of CO2 and that of the damage this tonne would do in the future. As concerns about climate change mounted, so did interest in this work. By 2010 Barack Obama’s administration had incorporated DICE into American policy. Mr Nordhaus’s climate economics had critics. He fought a long battle with Nicholas Stern, a British economist who favoured more weight on future

lives. Some climate scientists thought he underestimated the probability of catastrophic events and contested the conclusion of his DICE paper that the economically optimal outcome was to allow average global temperature to rise by 3°C above pre-industrial levels. Yet even as they challenged some of his numerical assumptions, his detractors happily worked within his framework. DICE made Mr Nordhaus a climate celebrity. But it was only the tip of a vast iceberg of academic output. A paper from 1975 suggested that politicians loosened monetary policy to bring down unemployment in the months before elections to win votes—and led to a proliferation of similar political-economy studies. In 1996 he compared the cost of generating the equivalent of an hour’s worth of light from a modern lightbulb over the ages. His conclusion, that it plummeted from 5.4 hours of a worker’s labour in 1800 to less than half a second in 1992, implied that conventional price indices vastly understate gains in living standards. He eventually returned to the preoccupation of his doctoral days. In 2005 he used American records from 1948 to 2001 to calculate that inventors got just 4% of the value their inventions brought to society. The economic contributions of innovators, in other words, far exceeded their fortunes. The paper appeared as America was minting new tech billionaires, and its arguments were embraced by their defenders. If the self-made tycoons give so much to society, why tax them more? Even progressive Californians seem to agree: in midterm elections on November 3rd most of the state’s voters appear to have rejected a ballot measure to tax billionaires’ wealth. Above all, Mr Nordhaus’s economics was a triumph of pragmatism. He believed in moulding messy problems into understandable frameworks—and embracing the implications, regardless of where they took you. Producing numbers that were useful, he thought, was sometimes more important than being precise. The best economic predictions, he argued in a paper in 1987, are the ones that incorporate the most information (his own informed projection made a decade before, that global temperatures would have risen by about 1°C by around now, was on the money). He imparted openmindedness to his students at Yale, too, patiently taking their questions after lectures—often for hours given how packed these got.

In another display of enlightened generosity, he presented the Nobel museum with the ancient Babylonian lamp he procured while investigating the cost of light. Beside his intellectual gifts to economics, this was but a flicker. ■ This article was downloaded by zlibrary from https://www.economist.com/finance-and-economics/2026/11/05/william-nordhaus- favourite-economist-of-the-right-and-the-left

Science & technology | Connecting the dots Astronomers are mystified by little red dots in the sky They could be the universe’s first supermassive black holes Nov 5th 2026 WHEN THE James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), a high-resolution camera orbiting the Sun, began sending images back to Earth in 2022, astronomers were greeted with something strange. Alongside the eclectic cast of cosmic characters they had been expecting—stars, galaxies, nebulae and the like—were a handful of small, red-tinted points of light, or, as the technical jargon has it, “little red dots”. Astronomers had no idea what they were. In looking for answers they have turned to the most mysterious objects in the universe—black holes. For some astronomers, the dots are black holes caught in unprecedentedly violent growth spurts. For others, they are bizarre, star-like objects with black holes at their centre. Getting it right matters: these dots could reveal the origins of the supermassive black holes

that sit at the centre of galaxies, and explain how they got so big so quickly. The debate is fierce, says Dale Kocevski, an astronomer at Colby College, in Maine. “I’ve never quite seen anything like this.” What is known is that the little red dots are, unsurprisingly, little—typically with a width less than 0.5% of the Milky Way’s—and red, emitting bright light that is rich in longer, redder wavelengths. They are also old. The universe’s first little red dots were born in the cosmic dawn 13.4bn years ago, when the universe was around 500m years old—not long after the first galaxies appeared. Since the dots were first discovered more than 500 of them have been reported, and the number continues to rise. “In every single image you’re taking, you’re seeing one of these objects stare right back at you,” says Rohan Naidu, an astronomer at the University of Hawaii who was part of the team that coined the term. “So they have to be important.” When astronomers first saw the little red dots, they thought the objects were the glow of very populous mature galaxies. But such well-developed galaxies would not have had enough time to form so soon after the Big Bang. That led some to dub the dots “universe breakers”, whose existence implied the universe might be older than previously thought. Then, in early 2023, Dr Kocevski and his colleagues discovered a unique pattern in the spectrum of light the dots produce. It suggested that they were surrounded by a cloud of dense, rapidly moving hydrogen gas. To get gas moving at the necessary speeds, huge gravitational forces would be needed. That disfavoured universe-breaking galaxies, as the large distances between their stars would keep the forces relatively small. Instead, a huge amount of mass would need to be concentrated in a tiny volume. That is the perfect description of a black hole. But this seems paradoxical. The gravity of black holes is so strong that not even light can escape their grasp: how, then, could they glow? The answer is that as matter falls into them, a process known as accretion, it emits radiation as it heats up. If a black hole is big enough, that radiation can be extraordinarily intense. For example, active galactic nuclei (AGNs)— accreting supermassive black holes at the centre of most galaxies—are among the universe’s brightest light sources.

Accreting black holes could thus be responsible for the brightness of the little red dots seen by the JWST. And although ordinary AGNs are surrounded by doughnuts of gas and dust, the heavy elements that make up the dust may not have had time to form so early in the universe’s history. “There may be so much gas that that doughnut becomes a cocoon,” says Dr Kocevski. “It completely envelops the thing.” Light interacting with the hydrogen gas would also come out reddened. But there is a problem. AGNs also release X-rays as the matter falling into them heats up. Little red dots, however, emit almost none at all. To explain this, the AGN advocates propose that the central black holes are accreting at an unusually high rate. Just as it is hard to get off a train as a crowd is scrambling to get on, the build-up of infalling matter limits how many X- rays can break through. For Dr Kocevski the appeal of this explanation comes down to Occam’s razor: “These are things we already know about, just slightly modified.” Dr Naidu, however, is in a more imaginative camp. “We just need to concede that this is a fundamentally new kind of astrophysical object,” he says. Dr Naidu and his colleagues propose that the little red dots are neither stars nor black holes, but something that shares features with both. Their imagination, however, does not extend to nomenclature: they call these novelties “black-hole stars”.
