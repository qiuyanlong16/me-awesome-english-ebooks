The prairie fire behind the Democrats’ big win Wins in Iowa and Kansas revived the left’s rural populism Nov 5th 2026 WHILE RUNNING for governor of Iowa, Rob Sand, a Democrat and the state’s auditor, reprised a campaign slogan used by Iowa Democrats of an earlier era: “It’s time to rotate the crops.” Voters agreed. Iowans elected Mr Sand to be their first Democratic governor in 15 years. Democrats won a Senate seat and flipped three congressional districts in the Hawkeye State, too. In Kansas, Adam Hamilton, the pastor of America’s largest United Methodist church, was the first Democratic senator elected by the state since 1932. Mr Hamilton’s victory is the most stunning and idiosyncratic of these results. He had the challenge of competing in a state that Donald Trump had won by 16 points in 2024. He had the benefit, however, of possessing ample charisma in a race against an opponent lacking it. The Republican

incumbent, Roger Marshall, a doctor, is a dismal campaigner. It didn’t help that he was plagued by reports that he sued his patients to collect medical debts. Across Democrats’ victories on the Plains, a few common themes emerge. Mr Hamilton was buoyed by the unpopularity of Donald Trump’s war with Iran, as were Democrats everywhere and those in farm states in particular. The war has increased the price of petrol, diesel and fertiliser—all crucial inputs for farms. Sarah Trone Garriott, a Lutheran minister who flipped a red district that contains liberal Des Moines and rural southern Iowa, yoked her Republican opponent to the president. One of her ads featured Mr Trump describing himself as “the best president for Iowa” and asking Iowans to re- elect the incumbent, Zach Nunn. That Mr Trump’s endorsement was used as an indictment of Mr Nunn reveals the extent of Iowans’ dissatisfaction with the man they have thrice backed in presidential campaigns. Democrats in the Plains were also able to harness surging national populism and give it a distinctly local character. Agrarian populism spread through the Plains during the Gilded Age, and reared again during tough times for farmers in the 1930s and 1980s. Josh Turek, Iowa’s Democratic senator- elect, calls himself a “prairie populist”. Mr Sand, incensed that Iowa’s Republican legislature curtailed his powers as auditor, campaigned for more transparency and less corruption. Sam Forstag, a millennial firefighting paratrooper, looks likely to win a House seat in western Montana. He preached economic populism and the need to get money out of politics— ideas that could hold appeal in a red state once home to New Deal Democrats and a strong labour movement. When pressed on policy, these candidates resemble Democrats in other states. Mr Turek denounces billionaires, wants to raise the minimum wage and reverse federal cuts to welfare. Yet they avoided alienating Republican voters by using distinctly post-partisan rhetoric and expressing discontent with the record of both major political parties. Mr Sand started his town halls by singing “America the Beautiful” and encouraging voters to clap for Republicans in attendance. Democrats in Middle America sometimes complain that their coastal strategists fall in love with rural candidates who seem straight out of “central casting”, without considering how authentic

those candidates appear to actual rural Americans. In this cycle, Kansans and Iowans seemed to see themselves in the Democratic challengers. That was not true for Dan Osborn, a populist independent who lost a Senate race in Nebraska. Unlike Messrs Turek and Hamilton, he seemed to have contempt for Trump voters. Leaked audio revealed him saying: “Billy Bob in fucking Red Cloud, Nebraska, who lives next door to the fucking Trump store as you pull into town, and is waving the Trump flag, all he knows is he’s on the winning team.” This year’s victories on the Plains will bolster Democrats’ hopes that the region offers more opportunities. Democrats have long bemoaned their loss of support in Iowa since the state helped launch Barack Obama to the presidency in 2008, then padded his win four years later.
