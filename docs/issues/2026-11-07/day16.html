The roadshow, coming three weeks after the pope underwent surgery for lung cancer, will draw big crowds. A bigger question is whether it can help win back Latin America’s waning faithful. The region’s educated young, in particular, are gradually going off organised religion. In the decade to 2020 the share of people around the world who say they do not identify with any organised religion—call them the “nones”—grew by a percentage point, to 24%. This shift may appear small, but behind it lies

much change. Every religion other than Islam stagnated or shrank over that period, when taking population growth into account. The share of people who say they have no religious affiliation declined slightly in Asia and barely budged in Africa or the Middle East. But it soared in Europe and the Americas. And of the three countries where the share of “nones” grew the most, two (Chile and Uruguay) are in Latin America. To see what this looks like on the ground, trek to Uruguay—the country in which Pope Leo is beginning his grand tour. Its constitution has long enforced an exceptionally strict division between church and state. Easter week, known as “Holy Week” in the rest of Latin America, is called “Tourism Week”. Christmas is dubbed “Family Day.” Not by chance, Uruguay legalised marijuana, gay marriage and abortion much earlier than its peers.
