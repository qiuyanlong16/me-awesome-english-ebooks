Donald Trump’s humiliation offers America an opportunity Voters demand a new way of doing things in Washington. Let them have it Nov 5th 2026 SURFERS CALL it the washing machine. When a giant wave throws you off your board and plunges you underwater, you lose all sense of where you are. That is what befell Donald Trump and his party on November 3rd, as they were slammed by tens of millions of American voters. They have still to grasp just how much their country has changed. Already it is clear this was not a normal midterm swing against a sitting president, but the biggest wave-election since the aftermath of Watergate more than 50 years ago. Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress, flipping seats deep into once-safe red waters, from South Carolina to Texas.

The coming days will spell out two further lessons. One is that this is not just a triumph for Democrats, but also for American democracy. Voters cannot be taken for granted. If they are angry, they will turn against parties they have long supported. The other lesson, which follows from this, is that both parties now need to find a new way of doing politics—one that spends less time riling up their constituents and more getting things done on their behalf. The wave has swept across America. With some races still uncalled, the Democrats have 54 Senate seats (including two independents) and around 260 in the House, up from 47 and 214. They are set to win the popular vote for the House by 13 points. They have also taken at least five more governors’ mansions. Kansas has its first Democratic senator since the New Deal. In Texas Democrats won their first Senate seat in almost 40 years and kicked out a three-term governor. It turns out that voters do not much like a president who is elected on a promise to lower prices and stay out of wars, but who then goes on to launch a tariff crusade and attack Iran—thereby driving up prices. And they hate it if that president’s relentless self-glorification and enrichment of his family and friends mocks their own hardship. In Mr Trump’s America, Gallup finds, nine in ten adults complain that corruption in their government is widespread—about as many as in Nigeria. The vote was thus a verdict on the president. But politicians from both parties should also reflect on voters’ rejection of the ugly mechanics of modern partisanship. Republicans gerrymandered seats, but failed to make gains. Elon Musk, a multi-billionaire, poured more than $150m into a race in Texas, but his candidate was trounced. Republican candidates stoked fears over Muslims and transgender people, and lost. One in Ohio backed an ad in which a mother damned his opponent: her own abused daughter. It sealed his defeat. The message from this is clear and it is hopeful. It has often seemed as if polarisation was stronger than the electoral feedback that ensures democratic accountability. Not this time. Voters demanded that politicians put the nation’s prosperity above the grubby pursuit of power. Just before the

election, polls say, 80% of Americans called for “substantial change” or “complete and total upheaval” in how America is run. Voters have done their job. Politicians must now do theirs. The immediate task falls to the Republicans. When Mr Trump took control of the party, he ditched its values. Most old-guard Republican politicians were either forced out of office or traded their principles for political survival. With their call for change, voters have given Republicans a second chance. Some may stick with the president, risking further drubbings in future elections. Braver souls will breathe new life into their party, even if that courts Mr Trump’s vengeance. He is more feared than loved. They may discover that, once the old king’s power begins to ebb, it drains away surprisingly fast. Plenty could go wrong—including with Democrats flushed by success. Their party is only a bit more popular than Mr Trump. Their campaign succeeded by pairing a promise to lower costs with one to crack down on corruption and incompetence. Both are vital, but the balance will be hard to strike. If Democrats ignore the rot, they will only deepen voters’ cynicism and despair at the federal government in Washington. But if subpoenas and impeachments consume all of their efforts and they fail to improve people’s lives, voters will feel that they have been hoodwinked once again. A running fight with the administration will also make it harder to pass legislation that would, say, restore funding for health care. Above it all will loom the legacy-obsessed figure of Mr Trump. Some hold out hope that he will spend the rest of his presidency pottering about the White House, gilding golf balls and opining on granite. That underestimates the president’s capacity, for good and ill. At home Mr Trump may focus on pardoning his friends and punishing his enemies. With only two years left, he and his cronies and ideologues may try to wring what more they can from their time in power, be that through lucrative state-supported mining ventures or more deportations. The president also retains enormous authority over foreign policy. He may yet pursue further strikes on Iran, try to bully Ukraine into a bad deal with Russia, or invade Cuba. As the White House’s more talented staff eye the

exits, a depleted executive branch may find itself even more subject to the president’s whims. It will be ill-prepared for a crisis. There is a glimmer of a possibility, however, that politicians will take their responsibility seriously. Republicans in Congress may consider working with Democrats, and vice versa (imagine that). They could pursue egregious corruption or even impeach the administration’s most reckless cabinet members, including Pete Hegseth, the secretary of war. They could develop rules for artificial intelligence that guard against disastrous outcomes without crippling innovation. Mr Trump is not a man who enjoys being left out. He may judge that he could improve his legacy by working with Congress, rather than trampling on it. That is not the likeliest scenario, we admit. El Salvador’s gangbuster tries his luck in Haiti The status quo is so dire Nayib Bukele might improve it. But the risks are huge Nov 5th 2026 Few places on Earth are as miserable as Haiti. The last elected president was tortured and shot dead in his home in 2021. Since then, gangs brandishing military-grade weapons have wrought havoc. They have taken over large swathes of territory in and around the capital, setting up roadblocks, terrorising residents and extorting “taxes” from them. At least 16,000 people, mostly civilians, have been killed, and over 1.5m displaced. Life expectancy is the shortest in the Americas. Ill-funded UN- backed forces sent to quell the violence have struggled. A UN “Gang Suppression Force” was approved in September last year, but only about 2,000 of the 5,500 soldiers it was promised have arrived, and they rarely venture out from their base.

Things are so bad that it is tempting to welcome almost any determined outsider who wants to impose order. Nayib Bukele, El Salvador’s president, has just sent over 2,000 Salvadoran soldiers to Haiti. His contingent outnumbers the UN’s forces. Mr Bukele is a brutal strongman but widely admired in the Americas for his crime-busting. Other countries have pledged logistical support for his intervention, including Colombia and the Dominican Republic, which shares an island with Haiti. President Donald Trump, who wants to see the region’s gangs smashed, appears to have given Mr Bukele the nod. Mr Bukele says his aim is to recover four Salvadoran air-force technicians who were working in the UN’s Gang Suppression Force when they vanished (and were perhaps killed) in an ambush last month. But 2,000 troops does not sound like a mere rescue mission. More probably, he wants to burnish his reputation, by showing that his record of suppressing gangs at home in El Salvador can be extended abroad. Mr Bukele once bragged that he was strong enough to fix Haiti. In social-media posts he boasts he can “finish off” its gangs. Foreign military interventions have a mixed record in Haiti. Previous UN efforts failed to calm the mayhem and sometimes added to it. A big worry about Mr Bukele’s mission is that its aims are unclear. Will the Salvadorans kill some gangsters and leave quickly, or occupy parts of the country? Will they work with other foreign forces and the local army and police? Today’s picture is confused, including not only the UN and the Haitian security services but also Vectus Global, a private military firm run by Erik Prince, an American with links to Trumpworld, who was hired by Haiti’s government to fight the gangs with drones. The UN frets that its role is being usurped. Mr Bukele is wrong if he imagines Haitian gangs are like their less scary counterparts in El Salvador. Back home Mr Bukele brought down the murder rate by locking up hordes of young men, almost 2% of the total population, without due process. (Trials, if they happen, are of hundreds of suspects at once.)
