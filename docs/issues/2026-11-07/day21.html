Will Poland’s war over the rule of law ever end? Liberals and populists are locked in an eternal struggle over the courts Nov 5th 2026 The scene outside Poland’s Constitutional Tribunal on November 3rd, as the court met to approve its draft budget, was unusually rambunctious. Hundreds of right-wing protesters gathered around a stage flanked by Polish flags and a large picture of the Virgin Mary. Speakers hurled abuse at the European Union; at Donald Tusk, Poland’s prime minister; and at the “usurpers” of the court—that is, the judges, appointed by parliament, whom Mr Tusk had helped to take their seats, despite obstruction by his populist- right opponents. The latest episode in the sorry saga of Poland’s top court began on October 30th, when the judges marched into the court to assume their duties while police sealed the area. Approved by parliament between March and September, they had until then been blocked by the court’s president,

Bogdan Swieczkowski, and Poland’s president, Karol Nawrocki, both aligned with the opposition Law and Justice (PiS) party. Mr Nawrocki is by law required to attend justices’ swearing-in ceremonies, but he had refused, giving Mr Swieczkowski an excuse to claim they were not legitimate. One of the new justices, Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, promptly announced she was suspending Mr Swieczkowski for a month and taking his place. Protesters and opposition leaders gathered outside, calling it a coup. Mr Nawrocki cut short a trip to Senegal in mid-air, flew back to Warsaw and forced his way into the building. By November 3rd the situation had calmed and the budget meeting went ahead, but judges named by the previous PiS- led government boycotted it. The struggle over the court goes back to 2015, when Mr Nawrocki’s predecessor, also a PiS ally, refused to swear in three judges chosen by the outgoing parliament. The new PiS government then packed the court with its own partisans. That led to years of wrangling in European courts, and the EU blocked billions of euros in funds. Since Mr Tusk and his Civic Coalition (KO) returned to power in 2023, parliament has picked eight judges to fill vacancies. Mr Nawrocki refuses to swear in five of them.

Four former presidents of the tribunal have defended Mr Tusk’s move. The European Commission, agreeing with opinions issued by European legal bodies, said the right to appoint the judges lay with parliament. But Ms Korwin-Piotrowska’s case for suspending the court’s president is shakier. Marcin Wiacek, a former national ombudsman, says he has “serious doubts” about its legality: it creates “a weapon any future government could wield”. Officials say there was no other way to break the deadlock. “Swieczkowski’s strategy was to hold the court hostage,” says Dariusz Mazur, the deputy justice minister. On October 31st Mr Tusk said that there can be “no compromise” in restoring the rule of law. But the suspensions are sure to be challenged in Polish courts, and could then reach European ones. After a decade of lawfare, the court’s credibility is shot. In late 2025 the EU’s top court ruled that it no longer met the requirements of an independent tribunal. Fought over by two camps, one headed by the president, the other by the prime minister, the court has become a political trophy. The tug-of-war will probably continue until parliamentary elections, scheduled next year. In trying to undo the knot entangling the court, Mr Tusk has tightened it further. Mr Nawrocki, meanwhile, can simply play for time. PiS may return to power in 2027 in a coalition with even more extreme populist parties, and try to oust the new justices. Many Poles care little for either side’s legal arguments, and ask only for a functional court. They are helpless, frustrated bystanders. Some analysts and legal scholars believe that only a complete “reset” can salvage the tribunal. That would require a political compromise between PiS and KO, as well as constitutional changes. France’s unhappy countryside turns to the populist right In long-suffering villages, Marine Le Pen has come to stand for hope Nov 5th 2026 Across the Gironde estuary from the chateaux of Haut-Médoc lie the less fashionable farmlands of the region around Bordeaux. They have been triply battered of late: by extreme heat, higher energy costs and a global decline in the drinking of everyday wine. Many farmers have given up. Blackened piles of vines lie in fields ready to be burned. This year 1,095 winegrowers in the Gironde have ripped out 7,825 hectares (19,336 acres) of vines. Over a quarter of the region’s vineyards have been destroyed since 2023. In the city of Bordeaux, rioters have set barricades ablaze in recent weeks. But here in the countryside France’s discontent is quieter and less visible. In the town of Blaye, pupils blockaded their secondary school, but it passed off calmly; on a weekday after school teenagers can be found playing cards at

pavement tables in the Bar des Sports. The feeling is distress rather than rage. Locals are sceptical about the future and worried about the cost of filling the tank in a place where a car is essential, and think the government is doing nothing to help. “I’m sad, not angry,” says a vineyard owner, who is packing it in after 30 years due to the collapse of exports to China and of wine-drinking in France, adding: “We have a president who doesn’t listen.” For decades rural Gironde tilted to the left, while the centre-right ruled in the prosperous city of Bordeaux. For 20 years the parliamentary constituency around Blaye was held by a Socialist, before swinging to the centrist party of President Emmanuel Macron in 2017. At parliamentary elections in 2022, however, the seat was captured by Edwige Diaz of Marine Le Pen’s populist-right National Rally (RN). Two years later she was re-elected in the first round, with 53% of the vote. In September the 39-year-old Ms Diaz, whose grandfather was a Spanish Communist, was elected to the Senate. “We are permanently campaigning here,” says Ms Diaz. “All year round we are at the markets, people see us, they know us.” She has driven herself to a small village for the retirement party of a local gendarme. At the wine bar opposite the church she meticulously greets customers one by one. The RN’s support now comes not only from farm labourers and the working class but from shopkeepers, pharmacists and other traders. At legislative elections in

2024 the party was the most popular countrywide among working-class voters (57% of whom backed it) as well as white-collar employees (44%). “The left has become more radical and defends Gaza more than farmers,” declares Ms Diaz. “People also know they have nothing left to hope for from Emmanuel Macron or his dauphins. Next to that, Marine Le Pen’s discourse is serious and reassuring.” Once a champion of the politics of indignation, Ms Le Pen has begun to leave the fury to the populist-left Jean-Luc Mélenchon and his Unsubmissive France (LFI) party. These days the RN leader is making a pitch, however improbably, as the candidate of comfort and promise. At her big campaign speech in the city of Orléans on October 25th, she dressed in a powder-pink jacket and spoke about “beauty” and “hope”. A local focus on improving daily life has enabled the RN to put down roots in unlikely places. The village of Saint-Savin has seen better days; two of its tattoo parlours are boarded up. For 12 years the village was run by the left, but in March for the first time voters elected an RN mayor, Frédérique Joint. She has turned the street lighting back on at night. In October the village held a three-day fête, complete with a torchlit procession from the church, soapbox-racing through the streets, a DJ and la country dancing. “With the loss of the vineyards, we’re losing our terroir, our savoir-faire, our traditions,” says Ms Joint. Local budgets are squeezed by higher prices in the supermarket and at the pump. “People have a need to get together at festive feel-good events.” There is plenty of red-meat identity politics at play, too, in places like this, with local candidates appealing to Christianity and the preservation of tradition. But the populists’ turn towards mundane governance issues offers some hope in hard times, and is part of a broader normalisation. “We are witnessing a major shift,” wrote Luc Rouban, a political scientist at Sciences Po university, in a recent research note, “with LFI taking the RN’s place on the fringes…LFI frightens moderate left-wing voters far more than the RN frightens moderate right-wing voters.” In some ways France’s anxious, bitter mood is perplexing. The country provides its citizens with an unusually protective cradle-to-grave welfare state. Public social spending, at 32% of GDP, is higher than in any other

European Union country but Finland. The French on average retire earlier and live longer as pensioners than most of their neighbours. Yet something has broken in voters’ trust in government and in their faith in the social contract. Confidence in mainstream politics is collapsing. Just 16% of all respondents told a poll in October that Mr Macron is a good president, a record low since he was elected in 2017. In another, 81% held the government responsible for the recent violence. Consumer confidence has fallen to its lowest for 13 years. After a decade in office Mr Macron and the centrist politics he ushered in no longer represent promise, but disappointment. One winegrower in the Gironde says simply that he feels “defeated”.
