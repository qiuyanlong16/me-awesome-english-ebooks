Is it time to end the era of remote work? A new book makes a case for getting out of your tracksuit and into your officewear Nov 5th 2026 Those mulling the current AI boom should look back to a kitchen in Google’s office in the early 2000s. Two employees, Noam Shazeer and Georges Harik, began to chat about how computers differentiated between essential and superfluous information. This chance encounter windingly led to the mechanism that underpins large language models. Had Google allowed remote work, Mr Shazeer and Mr Harik might never have spoken. In-person spitballing has led to ideas ranging from the hugely consequential (IBM’s personal computer) to the hugely entertaining (“Finding Nemo”). That is one reason why bosses often lament the rise of remote work, notes a new book by Natalia Emanuel and Emma Harrington,

two economists. In 2019 American workers spent 7% of their paid days working at home. Now it is about a quarter. Studies suggest that remote work may increase the quantity of output while decreasing its quality. In 2025 a review of return-to-office mandates at large banks found that when firms called analysts back to the office for a few days each week, the accuracy of their earnings forecasts leaped 15% compared with peers at banks that remained fully remote. What is more, remote work tends to dent the sort of tasks that rarely show up in measures of output. Senior software engineers write more code from the comfort of their homes, but they review junior employees’ work less comprehensively. Lost mentorship and undiscovered ideas can affect the future of a firm. Such losses may be why the shift has stung new graduates in particular. A new study by Peter John Lambert of the London School of Economics and Yannick Schindler of the University of Oxford suggests remote work, not AI, is to blame for young jobseekers’ woes of late. Jobs done entirely online tend to be susceptible to being done remotely. Firms that are reluctant to let untested employees work away from their boss’s watchful eye may be choosing not to hire them at all. So why not cancel the corporate Zoom subscription and squeeze back into the cubicle? The authors note that some employees are well-served by remote work: for example, parents and people with disabilities. For most companies a mix is best. To help bosses settle on the right number of days in-office, “In Person” features a “calculator” that asks them to add and subtract days based on the firm’s characteristics. But what executive would not say the stakes of their business are high (add two days), or that innovation is crucial to their firm’s future (add three days)? When it comes to such decisions, psychologists may be more helpful than economists. Still, many of the book’s points are well-taken. How many days employees spend at the office may be less important than whether they overlap with key colleagues. Hot-desking is a scourge, as would-be collaborators sit together only occasionally. And if bosses want to reap the benefits of in-person culture, they also need to show face. “In Person” should contribute to an

ongoing conversation about why remote work has remained so persistent if it comes with so many drawbacks. Watercooler-chat lovers, rejoice. ■ This article was downloaded by zlibrary from https://www.economist.com/culture/2026/11/05/is-it-time-to-end-the-era-of-remote- work

Chinese tech helps repressive African governments crush dissent A fascinating new book investigates the cause and effects of China’s investment in Africa Nov 5th 2026 WHEN CHINA paid $200m to build a new headquarters for the African Union (AU) in Addis Ababa (pictured), it was perceived as an act of great generosity. But five years later technicians noticed something odd. Late every night the servers, which had been provided by China, whirred furiously. Data was being exported to servers in Shanghai, including confidential emails, diplomats’ memos, security-camera footage and more. Algerian and Ethiopian specialists who later swept the building reportedly found microphones hidden under desks and inside walls. The Chinese government and the companies involved, Huawei and ZTE, all denied any wrongdoing. No surprise there. More dismaying was the public

reaction of African leaders, who voiced fury not over the surveillance but towards the reporters who helped expose it. In a gripping new book, Paul Kenyon, a British journalist, argues that China is the latest external power out to gouge the continent while empowering corrupt, local power-brokers. His broader warning is that much of Africa is being taken over by Chinese tech. Many autocratic governments are using Chinese systems to monitor their people, especially dissidents. That means snooping via mobile-phone networks installed by Chinese firms, for example. Think of the methods of information control in Xinjiang being applied in Zimbabwe, Kenya and beyond. Mr Kenyon, who has a good eye for detail but a tendency to overstate his case, alleges that Chinese engineers in Africa are the “foot soldiers of China’s digital colonialism, enabling totalitarian regimes to prosper in Africa because China wants them to”. China’s tech is spreading rapidly across Africa thanks partly to generous loans, which make it cheap. Much of it is used to hamper free speech and browsing. A Chinese company, Geedge, exports a version of China’s Great Firewall, for example, so African governments can try to censor what citizens see online. Nasty regimes are the keenest to learn from China. In Kampala, the hilly capital of Uganda, white poles have been erected; they are topped with some 5,000 cameras that feed images to 83 monitoring centres. This is presented to the public as a measure to cut crime: the monitors use AI, facial recognition and biometric analysis to track individuals. But it is also a tool for a repressive government: Yoweri Museveni, the president, can monitor those who support the opposition. For years America, Britain and other rich countries have shunned contracts with Huawei and ZTE, citing security risks. No such hesitancy exists in Africa, where few governments could afford to risk damaging relations with a big trading partner. China has also provided loans for countries, including Algeria and Nigeria, to pay for satellites that it built and launched. In a related corner of the contest for information, Mr Kenyon points to the surging number of Chinese communications staff, whom he calls “propagandists”, setting up in Africa. Xinhua, China’s official state news

agency, has around 35 bureaus on the continent, more than any other news firm. What is the end-game? China is pursuing Africa’s natural resources, including its abundant oil reserves and rare earths. But it is also trying to win political support for its challenge to Western values. China may succeed in creating a “splinternet” in Africa, with digital interaction happening only in tightly monitored spaces. This would be a “sterile world” with no dissent, Mr Kenyon argues. It would also mark a change from today when, in order to shut up opposition voices, governments sometimes cut off millions of people from online services for months or even years. “China’s African Empire” is a clear-headed warning that, as with the AU building, China is not necessarily the benign partner it claims to be.
