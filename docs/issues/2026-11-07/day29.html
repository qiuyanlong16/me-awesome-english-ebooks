Is curiosity all it’s cooked up to be? A quality that everyone lauds has its complexities Nov 5th 2026 In any DISCUSSION about the human qualities that will matter to employers in an AI-inflected future, a few traits recur: taste, judgment, empathy, expertise. One of these bingo-card words is curiosity. Curious people are more likely to explore the contours of new technologies, runs the argument, and to master new skills. If you want a workforce that is capable of adapting to rapid change, inquisitiveness is a superpower. It’s almost impossible to be against curiosity—which explains why job candidates tend not to brag about being totally uninterested in the world around them. But when everyone agrees on something, it’s usually worth wondering if there’s another side to the story. True, curiosity has lots of benefits. Intellectual curiosity is predictive of academic success, according to a meta-analysis by Sophie von Stumm of the

University of York and her co-authors. Inquisitiveness helps learning. A study by Matthias Gruber of Cardiff University and his colleagues found that people have better recall of information that they were eager to find out about (and even of things they had learned incidentally when they were in a state of heightened curiosity). Curiosity also aids creativity. In a paper by Lydia Paine Hagtvedt of Akamai Technologies and her co-authors, participants were asked to guess how Harry Houdini had performed an illusion in which he made an elephant disappear. Those who were told their answer was close but not correct did better on a subsequent challenge to think up new tricks than a control group who had been told they had guessed correctly. The itch to solve a puzzle is an effective way to get people exploring lots of different ideas. That same itch can benefit organisations in other ways. Curiosity hooks the interest of consumers. There must be better reasons to upgrade to LinkedIn’s premium tier, but none more powerful than wanting to see who on Earth keeps looking at your profile. If charity collectors solicit unusual amounts of money rather than the usual round numbers ($8.23, say, rather than $10), people appear to be more likely to donate. The specificity of the request piques interest, disrupting that rehearsed speech about needing to get to a meeting. A sense of mystery can also be used to screen for curious job candidates. Google once stuck a maths problem on a billboard without revealing that it was the one paying. Anyone interested enough to solve the problem was led to a recruitment site. Nonetheless, the intuition that curious employees must be wholly good for organisations is worth querying. For one thing, curiosity is inherently distracting. The first two paragraphs of this column, for example, contain one of the longest words in the English language whose letters appear in alphabetical order; a wildly curious person will start hunting for it now rather than reading on. Inquisitive colleagues pull on loose threads, asking ever more questions and requesting ever more data. They may find something valuable, or they may just delay a decision. Curiosity entails moving from your immediate focus to

something else that has drawn your attention, explains Spencer Harrison of INSEAD, a co-author of a paper on “productive inefficiency” that explores how short-term detours can lead to potential long-term pay-offs. Curiosity is also closely associated with autonomy. Participants in a study by Nicola Schutte and John Malouff of the University of New England were asked to watch a video. Those who had some choice of what to watch were more curious about its subject-matter than those who were assigned one. That raises questions for organisations about how much leeway employees should have to explore questions which interest them. In “The Scientific Mindset”, a new book, Stefan Thomke of Harvard Business School describes Booking.com’s culture of experimentation. The travel site is famous for allowing product teams to launch their own A/B tests of new features, roughly 1,000 of which are running in parallel at any one time. Such decentralisation would be a leap for many firms. An organisation that hires curious people is always going to be better off than one that selects people who are dead behind the eyes. But making the most of curiosity means accepting trade-offs—the time spent wandering down blind alleys, the experiments that go on largely out of sight. There’s almost nothing bad about curiosity. Electrification and the artificial-intelligence boom will keep lifting copper demand, she argues, yet new supply is scarce and slow to develop. In the past 15 months copper prices have surged by 40%,

bringing Freeport’s share price with them (see chart 2). That, combined with new technology and greater political support, leads Ms Quirk to suggest that America may be on the cusp of a copper version of the shale revolution. People once assumed that the country would remain dependent on imported oil and gas; instead it became a major exporter. Ms Quirk reckons her industry could see something similar. One day, perhaps. But the strategy now being pursued by America’s copper giant will contribute only modestly. Last month Freeport said it was aiming to lift its total output of the metal to 4.5bn-5bn pounds by 2030, up from a projected 3bn in 2026. Its approach is strikingly incremental. Freeport’s first priority is to restore output at its biggest mine—Grasberg, in Indonesia, which usually accounts for over 35% of its output (and is 51% owned by the Indonesian government). Since a giant mudslide in 2025 that killed seven workers, production there has been below par. Ms Quirk expects the site to have fully recovered by the end of 2027. Next is to selectively expand existing sites. Most advanced is a plan to double the capacity of Bagdad, an 81-year-old mine in Arizona. On November 4th Freeport said its board had approved the $4.5bn investment, which Will Tankard of CRU, a consultancy, describes as “low risk”. Freeport

says the project, which has all its permits, will break even at $4 a pound; copper trades near $6.40 today. Bagdad’s doubling could add up to 250m pounds a year of copper supply by 2030. Freeport is also studying another mine expansion in Arizona, and seeking permits for one at El Abra in Chile, though neither will start before the next decade. The final focus for Freeport is on new technology. At most of its mines it deposits ores that contain too little copper into giant stockpiles. These now hold about 40bn pounds of copper, says Ms Quirk. To extract more from that ore, Freeport is experimenting with new forms of “leaching”, which uses acid to dissolve copper from rock. Last month the miner said it had tested a solution in the lab that may let it recover 800m pounds of copper a year from the stockpiles by 2030, up from a current annualised rate of 220m. Trials are planned in the field next year. In 2025 Freeport has also converted Bagdad’s fleet to autonomous trucks. Ms Quirk says it plans to do the same elsewhere in America within the next few years to ease labour constraints, allowing it to produce more from existing mines. The company recently opened a centre in Phoenix to remotely perform dispatch work that used to be done on site.

For investors, however, Freeport has recently lost some of its lustre. From a peak in August, its share price has fallen by more than 20%.
