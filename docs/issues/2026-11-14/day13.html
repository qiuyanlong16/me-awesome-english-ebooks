American workers face a surge in health costs Employer-sponsored insurance, the backbone of American coverage, is becoming more frail Nov 12th 2026 Health care ranks high on Democrats’ agenda in Congress, as they seek to restore subsidies to buy individual insurance and reverse cuts to Medicaid, the health programme for the poor. However, a different problem looms, too. In the coming weeks, the 157m Americans who receive insurance through employers will have to choose a plan for next year. The cost of their insurance is due to rise by an average of 8% in 2027, according to Marsh, a consultancy, the biggest price hike in more than 20 years. Employer-sponsored health care has long been the backbone of American coverage. About half of Americans get their health insurance through their job, that of their spouse or their parents. But as health insurance gets ever more expensive, bosses are looking for ways to trim their spending, whether

through making workers pay for more of their care in cash, cutting coverage or even scrapping health benefits entirely. Already, six in ten Americans worry about affording health care, according to KFF, a health-research outfit, a higher share than worry about petrol. As employers strip back coverage, that anxiety is sure to rise. Because health premiums paid by employers are excluded from income and payroll taxes, the government incentivises bosses to compensate workers by offering them health insurance. However, employers are problematic middle men. An employer generally covers about 80% of an insurance plan’s cost, with staff paying the remaining 20% themselves, or an average of $7,500 for a family plan. Fast-growing insurance costs borne by employers limit workers’ salaries, usually in a way that is not transparent to staff. Health costs spiked in 2021 as Americans resumed the surgeries and treatments that they had put off during the covid pandemic. But in recent years, premiums have risen faster than inflation and nominal wages (see chart). There are many reasons for this. Years of consolidation have given hospitals greater pricing power. Workers are seeking more treatment for behavioural health and innovative cancer medicines are helping to drive up premiums,

too. GLP-1s, popular anti-obesity medicines, have probably pushed up premium costs by about a percentage point, according to Marsh. A similar share comes from new artificial-intelligence tools that help hospitals to spot extra charges that a human might miss. Some employers will shop around for different coverage, but all insurers face higher costs. So firms are changing their offer to staff. In 2026 roughly two in five large employers made workers pay a bigger share of their premiums, according to a survey by KFF. Next year, with costs rising even more quickly, monthly payment increases of around 10% are not out of the question, says Beth Umland of Marsh. Another option for employers is to restrict who qualifies for insurance. Next year Disney, a big entertainment firm, will rescind coverage from employees’ spouses if they can get insurance through their own employer, a step that is hardly draconian. Other firms may impose more potent restrictions, for instance by limiting patients’ choice of doctors and services. Starbucks has stopped covering GLP-1s for weight loss, with more firms due to follow suit: 10% of the companies now offering GLP-1s to treat obesity don’t plan to do so next year, according to the Business Group on Health, an advocacy group. Some employers may go further, eliminating health insurance completely. Small companies have been dropping coverage for decades, explains Paul Fronstin of the Employee Benefit Research Institute, a think-tank. In 2000 more than 80% of firms with 10-49 employees offered health insurance to their staff—now, just 59% do, a share that looks set to decline. The exchanges created by the Affordable Care Act, better known as Obamacare, offered individuals a new way to shop for insurance. However, premiums there are rising even more quickly. With unemployment still low, companies have tried to avoid severe cuts, lest they drive away needed workers. Should the job market loosen, they might trim health benefits further. Democrats such as Abdul El-Sayed, a senator- elect from Michigan, have revived calls for universal health coverage. More cuts to employer-sponsored insurance give them new ammunition for their argument. ■

Florida, long since evolved from being a swing state, is Republicans’ most defiant stronghold. There are three main reasons Florida was spared the uprising against Republicans. The first is demographics, an advantage that Republicans elsewhere will struggle to mimic. For six years Florida politicians have advanced policies, from rejecting covid lockdowns to enacting universal school vouchers, to make the state a sanctuary for MAGA refugees. Although Democrats turned out a higher share of their voters this year, they could not overpower Republicans, who came into the election with 1.5m more registered voters. It helped Republicans that Hispanics, who propelled swings in other red states, were less keen on Democrats in Florida. Cubans make up half the state’s Hispanic voters and, unlike Mexicans or Puerto Ricans, have been fused to the Republican Party since the days of Ronald Reagan. Exit polls show they moved left but didn’t flip. The second reason for Republicans’ success is that—unlike in Texas, Kansas or Iowa— Democrats in Florida ran mediocre candidates who struggled to raise money. In the race to succeed Mr DeSantis, who was term-limited, Byron Donalds, the Republican nominee, outraised David Jolly, his Democratic opponent, $173m to $27m; Mr Jolly managed his own campaign, refused to debate and, insiders say, was allergic to asking for cash. But whereas James Talarico in Texas and Annie Andrews in South Carolina campaigned on messages of unity, Ms Nixon was known for yelling at her Republican colleagues in the legislature with a pink bullhorn. In the general election she succeeded in driving turnout, including of voters who opposed her. National Democrats refused to cough up much cash themselves, both because they doubted some candidates and because running ads in Florida is so expensive—supporting a statewide candidate means placing ads across ten media markets and appealing to an electorate so diverse it can feel like Manhattan and rural Alabama stitched together.
