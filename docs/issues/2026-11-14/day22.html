The prime minister’s counterparts in Brussels may have thought this an unserious basis on which to negotiate. The cabinet is split, as is Parliament. Mr Burnham sounds nonchalant. As he breezily remarked to The Economist: “Who knows how it plays out in the endgame here?” Yet Britain’s prime minister has won a hearing in Brussels. Few there think rejoining is imminent, and it is not at the top of anyone’s in-tray. But Mr Burnham’s is getting credit for breaking an omertà in Westminster that has held since 2020, when the Brexit divorce was finalised. His musings appear to Eurocrats as if he is probing the limits of British public opinion. It is quite a contrast with Sir Keir Starmer, his predecessor, who spent most of his time

in Brussels stubbornly ruling things out. “It’s not just an internal Labour matter, no,” says a European diplomat. “It is something big, the start of a debate about rejoining.” At a summit on November 20th, Britain and the EU are expected to finalise a package facilitating youth mobility and trade in food and emissions. In a speech around then, Mr Burnham plans to sketch out options for a long-term relationship. He insists he is open-minded about the possible model. But recently he has defended the free movement of people, once a taboo, and pointed out the deficiencies of rule-taker status. Join the dots and they point towards Britain rejoining the EU. The electoral gambit is obvious: a promise to get closer to Europe may help patch up Labour’s splintered coalition. But British manifesto pledges (if that is what Mr Burnham’s musing becomes) have a habit of landing on Brussels’s doorstep.
