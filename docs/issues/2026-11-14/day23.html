Sunderland, once the world’s biggest shipbuilding hub, has been in decline for decades. Its last shipyard closed in 1988. Tucked on England’s north-east coast, the city of 290,000 sits third-bottom for earnings in a ranking of Britain’s 63 largest urban areas. It is dead last for businesses per person. Voters showed their discontent at local elections in May, handing the populist-right Reform UK 58 of the council’s 75 seats. Labour went from 54

councillors to five. “People still are not happy, even with all of this,” laments Mr Miller. Might e-sports help give Sunderland a brighter future? Their venture has set up shop in what was a vacant Audi dealership next to Sunderland Football Club’s Stadium of Light. It has also turned a row of town houses and connected office space into its National Esports Performance Campus, where touring gaming pros can stay. The former car dealership has been transformed into a 15,000-square-foot (1,400 square metres) e-sports venue, dubbed the British Arena. (“We couldn’t believe no one had used the name before,” says Mr King.) On a clear Thursday evening in October the arena hosted its second event, relaying a Sunderland away match on its giant screen. The 250-seat venue is barely 100 metres from the 49,000-seat stadium, which provides a handy source of footfall on match days—and also highlights the gulf between e- sports and traditional ones. Mr King wants the British Arena to be more than just an entertainment venue. He hopes it will help elite gamers learn to perform to a crowd, while supporting careers in hospitality, broadcasting and coaching, too. Being the home of British Esports will build Sunderland’s reputation as a forward-looking digital city, enthuses Liz St Louis, the local authority’s aspirationally titled director of smart cities and enabling services. Sunderland Council is not the only part of the British state putting faith in e- sports. So are the armed forces, aware that Ukraine’s battlefields have swarmed with drones piloted by youths with PlayStation controllers. The British Arena’s first official event was the International Defence Esports Games, at which Canadian personnel beat France in Counter-Strike 2 and Britain took the Rocket League title.

Where once the armed forces recruited from the playing fields of Eton, they now see value in gamers. The Ministry of Defence formally recognises e- sports and last year installed a room of gaming PCs on the Royal Navy’s flagship aircraft-carrier. Britain’s super position in quantum technology Some of the industry’s bets are already paying off Nov 12th 2026 Quantum computing is a bit like the strange laws of physics it relies on: scary enough to give national-security wonks nightmares, yet still sufficiently puny for sceptics to dismiss. Perhaps no country wants it to work more than Britain. Three of the world’s top five quantum clusters are on its soil, reckons the European Centre for International Political Economy, a think-tank. On November 6th at a showcase in London, Sir Peter Knight, a pioneer in quantum technology, reiterated a goal to make a quantum computer that can outperform supercomputers in Britain by 2035. Yet even now other quantum technologies are proving their worth. Mostly this is down to quantum sensing, which exploits the sensitivity of quantum systems to faint changes around them. Use cases in medicine have abounded for decades (MRI scanners, for instance); now they are

multiplying. A spinout from the University of Nottingham, Cerca Magnetics, makes brain-scanning helmets fitted with quantum sensors that can detect subtle magnetic fields. It has made an operating profit in each of the past five years. Others are experimenting with diagnostic tools for breast cancer, and finger-prick tests for diseases such as hiv. Another application for sensing is positioning, navigation and timing (PNT). Knowing where you are, where you are going and what time it is has always been useful for soldiers. The war in Ukraine, meanwhile, has shown how easy it is to jam satellite systems such as gpS. So the Royal Navy has been trialling quantum navigation with compact atomic clocks, which do not depend on signals from space. Similar uses stretch beyond the battlefield. Britain’s latest national risk register identifies the loss of pnt, whether from technical failure or a cyber- attack, as a “significant” risk. It recommends that private firms keep “resilient backup systems”—such as atomic clocks. Days before the register was published, in July, a nationwide network outage in Australia demonstrated the potential for disruption. Faulty software from Telstra, the country’s biggest telecoms company, halted trains and payments and blocked some emergency calls. The British government’s budget in October included £138m ($185m) for quantum-sensing kit to be used for defence, health care and transport. Useful as brain scanners and atomic clocks may be, they are merely heralds in the march of quantum tech. The highest hopes are pinned on quantum computers’ mathematical powers. Large-scale machines would be able to do some sums vastly faster than today’s supercomputers, transforming computational chemistry and materials science. Subodh Kulkarni, the boss of Rigetti, an American firm that operates in Britain, thinks that running quantum processors alongside the artificial-intelligence chips used today will make it quicker and cheaper to train large language models. For now, quantum computers’ error rate is too high for them to be reliable. British startups are working to fix this: Riverlane is building error-correction systems, while Phasecraft is developing algorithms that can do useful work even on error-prone machines. Through ProQure, a £1bn procurement

competition, the government is supporting promising firms and helping validate their prototypes. The most advanced machines will in all likelihood be built in the “golden triangle” spanned by Cambridge, London and Oxford, home to hardware firms such as Quantum Motion and orca.
