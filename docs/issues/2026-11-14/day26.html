Polling firms must play methodological whack-a-mole, correcting errors from the previous election and anticipating future mistakes. In recent years discussion has been dominated by the issue of “non-response bias”: the idea that those who do not want to be polled lean in a particular direction politically. As politics in many Western countries has become less about economics and more about cultural issues, educated voters—who are more likely to respond to polls—have moved towards centre-left parties. A team of political scientists including Messrs Jennings and Wlezien found that polls have tended to overestimate left-wing parties by an average of 1.4 percentage points globally. In America, this effect has been compounded by Republican scepticism of the institutions, such as universities and the media, that often commission or produce polls. In the past three American presidential elections, polls substantially underestimated support for Donald Trump. In its poll in October of the Senate race in Kansas, the Washington Post reported that 32% of Democrats responded to its survey, compared with only 12% of Republicans. The large gap suggests not only that Republicans were underrepresented, but also that the few who did respond may not have been representative. Along with non-response bias, online pollsters have to contend with “low- quality” respondents, who are more interested in bagging the reward for completing the survey than in giving their opinions. Mr Page compares the struggle to weed out such participants to drone warfare between Russia and Ukraine, with each side innovating just as fast as the other. “Polling is increasingly about statistical modelling as much as sampling,” says Mr Jennings. One of the most successful polling initiatives of the midterm elections was a collaboration between the New York Times and Siena College. This year the team adopted an incredibly complicated system of survey weights which involved estimating the left-right position of every registered voter in the country, to ensure polls were ideologically representative. On top of that, rather than weighting for each demographic characteristic separately, the pollsters created a system that assesses

respondents by how unusual their unique combinations of characteristics were, compared with the population (“energy balancing”). The previous method would have overestimated the winning margins of Democrats who won Senate races by three points, on average. The new system showed almost no bias. So what were pollsters doing wrong in America and Brazil? “Mobilisation was a huge component of the result for both the first round and the run-off, like never before,” says Mr Roman of Brazil’s election. “Voters didn’t show up for Lula in the first round, and they really showed up on a mass scale in the run-off.” After Mr Bolsonaro’s victory in the first round, centre-left voters realised that he might really become president. Lula’s supporters were energised, particularly on social media. Overall turnout rose from 119m to 124m, with larger increases in municipalities won by Lula, especially in big cities. In the end he won with 52% of the vote—four points higher than his share in pre-election polls. Surveys also seem to have misjudged turnout in America’s midterms. The Economist analysed seven polls released in October that included a detailed breakdown of the composition of their sample. Respondents who said they had voted for Kamala Harris in 2024 outweighed those who had plumped for Donald Trump by two percentage points. That number already indicates some turnout adjustment on the part of pollsters: in reality she lost the election by 1.5 points. However, exit polls suggest that the actual electorate this November was even bluer, having preferred Ms Harris by five points. If you retrospectively adjust the polls to account for this, they go from showing Democrats with a 9.5-point lead to 11.5, explaining around half of the polling error (Democrats won by 14 points). For Democrats, this enthusiasm advantage was partly driven by black voters, especially in the South, who helped elect Democratic senators in deep-red South Carolina and Mississippi. The New York Times estimates that they made up 24% and 33% of voters in those two states respectively, up by three percentage points compared with 2024. Elsewhere, Hispanic voters showed up to vote in greater numbers than in previous midterm elections. Meanwhile, many Republicans stayed at home. In counties and districts won by Ms Harris with more than 80% of the vote in 2024, turnout in the midterms was around 81% of that in the presidential election. In those where

Mr Trump won with more than 80% of the vote, the figure was 12 percentage points lower. The elections expose a particular weakness of opinion polls. “My general view is that surveys are totally crappy these days about getting non-voters,” says Mr Rivers. By their nature, those who respond to polls are more likely to be highly engaged, and more likely to vote, than those who do not. At the same time, respondents are optimistic (or dishonest) when answering questions about their likelihood to vote. Unlike demographic questions, which are fairly consistent over time and which respondents tend to answer accurately, turnout is fluid. “The typical approach would be to ask people on a scale of one to ten how likely you are to vote,” says Mr Page. “But if you get a big increase in the number of people who say they are eight, nine or ten, do you believe them or not?” Despite this weakness, the polling misses also show that pollsters are striving to improve their estimates. In Brazil polling firms re-weighting their surveys after the first round to reflect the electorate that voted for Mr Bolsonaro. This was methodologically sound, even though it led them to overstate his support in the second round. Meanwhile, American pollsters were trying to correct their errors in the election of 2024, when they underestimated Republicans (see chart 2).

The Economist’s forecast of this year’s congressional elections aimed to anticipate polling errors, based on historical data. Our model incorrectly judged that pollsters were overestimating the Democrats, since projected landslides tend to underwhelm. It also judged that the low share of undecided voters in this year’s polls was a good sign for Republicans, because undecided voters tend to vote against the president’s party in midterms. These factors meant that the central forecast of our model compounded the pollsters’ errors, rather than remedying them. The similarities in the polling misses in America and Brazil might lead some observers to predict the same skew in polling of other elections. Optimistic centrists might, for example, conclude that polarising right-wing candidates will always galvanise centre-left voters to defy the polls. Indeed, that is the hope of French centrists, who are looking warily towards next year’s presidential election. Marine Le Pen, the leader of the populist-right National Rally, leads opinion polls of the first round with around 35% of the vote, on average. She also leads most hypothetical polls of the second round. In the past two presidential elections, Emmanuel Macron, France’s centrist president, defeated Ms Le Pen soundly, with a slightly higher vote share than polls had predicted.

However, the historical evidence for repeated polling errors is thin, let alone across different countries, or in presidential elections with different candidates. Indeed, the second-placed candidate in most polls is a left-wing firebrand, Jean-Luc Mélenchon, with around 17% support. He is one of the most polarising and unpopular figures in France, whereas voters have warmed to Ms Le Pen since 2022. A contest between the two is unlikely, therefore, to inspire a surge of voters determined to foil Ms Le Pen. What is more, French pollsters may have learned from their previous mistakes, or even overcorrected. Though a polling error is certainly possible, relying on one is never a good idea. (The Economist’s forecast model of the French election will be published next year.) Likewise, social democrats hope that Pedro Sánchez—a left-wing escape- artist who is prime minister of Spain—can repeat his poll-defying feat in 2023 and cling to power. That year, polls underestimated his Socialist Party (PSOE) by around three percentage points. It was just enough to cobble together a coalition of leftist and regionalist parties to stay in power. The Economist’s poll tracker for the election on November 29th shows 27% support for PSOE, just behind the centre-right People’s Party on 31%. However, since 2023 Mr Sánchez’s coalition partners have taken a tumble and the populist-right Vox has gained ground. The combined right-wing parties are polling at 50%, so should secure a majority under Spain’s electoral system. The left trails on 35%, with regional parties on 9%. There is still time for public opinion to shift, but if the polls hold, only an unusually large error would save Mr Sánchez. Next year anxious poll-watchers will no doubt hope for a polling miss that favours their preferred candidates in Poland and Italy, too. However, there is no sign that polling errors are growing, nor that their slight past skew to the left has become a bias to the right. An average absolute polling error of two points does not preclude the possibility of a big miss but, given the ever deeper potential pitfalls, is remarkably accurate. From early in the midterm campaign, the polls clearly indicated a big win for Democrats and a swing from Latinos in particular. That the polling averages foresaw the biggest blue wave in a generation, rather than two, seems forgivable. Most important, polls look better than the alternatives.
