Russia’s aim is not to convince adversaries to believe its twisted version of the truth. Since Soviet times, it has specialised in deniable attacks that leave others doubting whether anything is true.

So far, so roguish. Such agents of chaos as Russia and Iran have long relished their ability to keep stability-loving rivals off-balance. Today, the unwelcome shock involves America and China playing the same game. Not long ago those two giants saw an interest in upholding a stable and predictable global trading order (while bending its rules as needed). Now the two countries seem to like ambiguous rules and hard-to-enforce agreements that leave trade partners dependent on their goodwill. To be sure, statesmen have been masters of ambiguity since they wore robes and swords, and carved treaties on stone tablets. In the modern era, what is more, America had always been careful to avoid offering hard-and-fast security guarantees to allies, lest they started foolish conflicts, believing that Uncle Sam would come to their aid. President Donald Trump is different. He breaks new ground by treating inconstancy as his superpower. This is true in foreign policy, as he threatens to bomb countries before offering them friendship in the next breath. It is the case in trade policy, as he adjusts tariff rates and export controls on a whim. When Trump aides are asked how foreign governments or investors are supposed to agree to big, long-term deals if the terms may later change, they serenely reply that “there is a value to uncertainty.” In their telling, doubt usefully reminds foreigners that access to America’s market is a privilege, not a right. They note the advice of the president himself: if outsiders wish to avoid tariffs, all they need to do is make things in America. In Trumpworld, American dominance is enhanced when foreigners do not know where they stand. In the context of the Ukraine war, that might involve Europeans pleading for continued access to American-made weapons or the Starlink satellite-internet system owned by Elon Musk. In future, it might involve access to the best AI models. Foreign pleading is the pay-off. China is also breaking new ground. It spent decades tut-tutting at America’s high-handed and changeable ways. Officials in Beijing would accuse America of nagging trade partners to open their markets, only to turn round and enact export controls and other curbs before a foreign rival became too formidable a competitor. America changes the rules when it fears losing a game, they would grumble. Worse, America claims to enjoy a “long-arm

jurisdiction” to enforce its laws worldwide, as Chinese legal experts complained. The grumbling lasted until China became dominant in the production of vital industrial inputs and world-class technologies, from rare-earth elements to batteries. Just in time for Mr Trump’s uninhibited second turn at the presidency, China found itself in a position to retaliate in relative safety. In the past couple of years China has enacted its own export controls and long- arm laws to punish foreign companies if they obey American laws and sanctions that hurt China. If a multinational firm is now paralysed with doubt, not knowing how to balance laws in America, where it is listed, with those in China, where it sources important components, that is no accident. If companies wish to avoid the vaguely written, politicised rules that govern export licences for rare earths and the like, they can make products in China and export them as finished batteries or electric cars. The offer is not subtle: China-based factories have no trouble sourcing rare earths; dependence is a small price to pay. If carrots do not work, China has sticks to use on foreigners trying to de-risk and diversify away from dependence on its supply chains. Few companies have looked as unassailable as Nvidia in recent years. Its whizzy chips, together with its software and networking gear, made it indispensable to the artificial-intelligence boom, affording it an outsize share of the profits from the technology. Now Jensen Huang, who co-founded the company and has led it for over three decades, has weakened Nvidia to a degree that no rival could have hoped to achieve. On November 12th, as The Economist went to press, its board was reportedly preparing to oust him. Mr Huang’s unravelling has been swift. In a short memo on November 8th he confessed to an extramarital affair, apologising to his family and his

company. At the same time Nvidia’s board announced it had launched an investigation into whether he had “been the target of a foreign-intelligence effort”. The admission followed reports that he had been romantically involved with Alice Huang (no relation), a Chinese executive at Megaspeed, a Singaporean data-centre company that buys Nvidia’s chips. American officials suspect Megaspeed of smuggling banned chips into China—and believe Ms Huang to be a Chinese agent (a claim rejected by the Chinese government). Megaspeed, a “neocloud” that rents out access to AI processors, is suspected by American officials of operating across both routes.
