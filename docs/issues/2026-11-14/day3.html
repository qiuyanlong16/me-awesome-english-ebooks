For decades the ANC could count on the votes of black South Africans, who are 80% of the population, because of its role in ending apartheid in 1994. In the early years it also delivered the goods: water, electricity, housing and a safety-net. But over time its performance has flagged. Voters increasingly see the anc as just another party. They punished it for water leaks, power cuts and sewage- ridden streets. The Democratic Alliance (da), the second-largest party, is now only six percentage points behind the ANC, from almost 50 two decades ago. Previously derided as the “white party”, the da is now the largest party in five of the eight big cities—because it has a record of running cities competently. Depending on coalition negotiations, Helen Zille, a da veteran, could soon become Johannesburg’s first white post-apartheid mayor. You could hardly ask for a more encouraging sign that voters are moving on from racial politics. Yet there is much to worry about, too. Most South Africans are disillusioned with democracy. Only 35% of possible voters bothered to cast a ballot on November 4th. More than a fifth of black voters opted for black-nationalist parties with ideas that are more Mugabe than Mandela. These include uMkhonto weSizwe (mk), which will probably run Durban, the city with the busiest port in sub-Saharan Africa, and the Economic Freedom Fighters (eff). Dangerous grassroots movements, from anti-migrant vigilantes to white nationalists, pose a threat, too. To his credit Cyril Ramaphosa, South Africa’s president since 2018, acknowledges that his party is losing support. Other leaders, in Africa and elsewhere, instead rig elections and violently cling to power. Since 2024 at the national level he has led a Government of National Unity, in effect an anc-da coalition, that has brought some political stability and sensible reforms. But his tenure is entering its twilight. He will be replaced as party leader in 2027 and must leave the presidency no later than in 2029. He still has much to do to secure his desired legacy: as the disciple of Mandela who shored up South Africa’s graft-eroded institutions, reformed the economy and thwarted ruinous populism.

An urgent priority for the country is to run its cities better. That requires more local power-sharing among parties, just as they have learned to do nationally. The anc will have to get used to being, at times, a junior coalition partner. Some in the party would prefer to unite the so-called “black parties”, cutting deals with mk and the eff. But that would lead to much worse and more corrupt governance, which voters would soon notice. Smaller parties, like Actionsa, which won 16% of the vote in Johannesburg, also face a test of their seriousness. A political realignment is under way that brings some risks for the continent’s largest economy. But mostly the change is welcome: voters are rewarding those who govern better, and punishing those who don’t, just as the architects of the constitution hoped they would. When the anc failed to deliver a better life for all, voters looked for an alternative. The boss of Nvidia, the world’s leading chip designer and until recently its most valuable company, had an extramarital affair. His lover was an executive at Megaspeed, a little-known cloud-computing firm that has bought billions of dollars’ worth of Nvidia hardware. American officials think she is a Chinese agent, and that her Singapore-based company is a front for smuggling the politically sensitive chips into China. As we went to press, Nvidia’s board was meeting amid reports that it was planning to oust Mr Huang. If it had yet to find the courage to sack him, fresh allegations of another affair—this time with a direct report—will

surely help. His position is untenable, and he must go. The same is true for American officials implicated in the Megaspeed scandal. Mr Huang’s admission of infidelity on November 8th followed months of rumours about his relationship with Alice Huang (no relation). It remains unclear at what point Nvidia’s boss knew that his government thought her to be a Chinese agent, or when the affair ended. Nonetheless, he showed a catastrophic failure of judgment. Romance with a customer creates an obvious conflict of interest. And given his position as the steward of an American technology that China desperately covets, Mr Huang should have been extra wary. Instead, he lied publicly about the nature of their relationship as recently as last month. If it is true that he also had an affair with a subordinate, no self-respecting board could allow him to remain. And Mr Huang’s transgressions may prove to have been graver still. Questions swirl as to whether Nvidia’s chief counsel intervened last year in an investigation into Megaspeed by America’s Commerce Department, which is responsible for enforcing export controls. Mr Huang’s advocacy of looser chip controls, ostensibly to keep China hooked on American technology, now arouses suspicion, as does his championing of Chinese open-source models. Those who have argued that Mr Huang should stay reason that he is essential to Nvidia, and that Nvidia is essential to America’s lead over China in AI. Yet it is precisely because Nvidia is so important that he cannot continue as its chief executive. It is in America’s long-term interests that there be penalties for egregious misconduct at the top of firms which are crucial to national security. And for all Mr Huang’s undoubted brilliance, the interests of Nvidia’s shareholders would hardly be served by a scandal-ridden chief executive facing a merry-go-round of investigations, including by the FBI. That there is no obvious successor makes the board’s job unenviable, but not impossible. Mr Huang has been deeply involved in every aspect of his business, with as many as 60 executives reporting to him directly—a situation the board should not have allowed. No one has been prepared for the top job. Even so, an experienced executive such as Colette Kress, Nvidia’s finance chief, should be capable of stepping in to stabilise the business while a permanent successor, perhaps from outside, is appointed.

Mr Huang is not the only one to have shown dismal judgment in the Megaspeed debacle. The Commerce Department, under Howard Lutnick, has failed abjectly to stem the flow of America’s best chips to China. It appears to have allowed Nvidia to keep selling to Megaspeed even after concerns over smuggling were raised, and to have resisted calls by the Pentagon, the Treasury and others in the administration to blacklist the Singaporean firm. Like Mr Huang, Mr Lutnick has advocated weaker chip- export controls. Alas, the secretary of commerce is unlikely to resign. But as Democrats take control of Congress, they will interrogate not only Mr Lutnick but also President Donald Trump, who has bought Nvidia shares and tapped the company to fund his new ballroom. For two years incoherent policy and lax enforcement have allowed China to gain ground in the race for a technology that may prove more powerful than nuclear weapons. With luck, that will now change. For years Mr Huang has been courted by governments and treated as a rock star. Like many powerful leaders, including others in Silicon Valley, he came to believe that he was too important for the normal rules to apply to him. The same attitude can be found, in even larger doses, within the Trump administration. This culture of impunity must end. It has damaged one of America’s great corporate successes and undermined its national security.
