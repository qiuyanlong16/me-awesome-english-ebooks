All that settled, he had to fight only local mafiosi, extortionists and property developers, one of whom got the Middleton Row clinic shut down. By then, though, finding sites was easier. And the Work had never stopped. His aim was simple: to eliminate the gaps in care in government hospitals. He was often the first call for accidents caused by traffic, trains, machinery and cooking fires, though the hardest cases, needing operations, had to be sent elsewhere. Lepers, a priority, were given long-term holistic care; eye and skin diseases were quickly diagnosed and treated. TB was the most common serious illness, much of it drug-resistant, though he could seldom afford the new drugs to deal with that. He monitored and hoped. His greatest success, he felt, was to set up the first free clinic for HIV in West Bengal. The condition was rife in the brothels of Nilmoni Mitra Street, where he lived in a single room hung with washing on the top floor of one of his schools. There was just space to put a chair, a table, a WC, a shower and a bed, and to swing a very small cat. At night he would hear the women screaming over their takings. Time then for a very small nip from a toothmug of his one luxury, Irish whiskey. It was this room that he left, in 2019, to return to England. At 88, his strength was fading. He had to admit, too, that for all his labours the needs in Kolkata were not much reduced. But it was better to light one candle than to sit and curse the darkness. It was better to install arsenic filters in two village wells, as Calcutta Rescue did in Malda District, than to let the whole region go on drinking poisoned water. It was better, too, to give a handful of people second-line drugs for their TB, rather than give none. The fact that anyone had them at all would give other sufferers hope. It astonished him how much spirit and resilience the poor of Kolkata had. As for why he had picked this work at all, he was still wondering. Sometimes, he reflected, you didn’t choose your life. Life chose you. He had wanted to be an Orthodox rabbi once, but after seeing his father die of a heart attack his faith fell away like an autumn leaf. On the farm he kept a beautiful old Bible on the kitchen table, and became Catholic for a while;

but it didn’t last. In India he began to believe in karma and reincarnation. From the positive bits in many religions he wove his own. In public, he had none.
