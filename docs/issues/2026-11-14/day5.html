By Invitation · By Invitation | Defenders, unleash thyselves!

AI sceptics focus on the wrong threats They overlook how vulnerable the status quo is and how much AI can help defenders, writes Anne Neuberger Nov 12th 2026 MUCH OF THE debate about artificial intelligence, including a recent leader in The Economist, asks how to prevent a powerful new technology from making the world more dangerous. There is an equally important, more urgent question: how quickly can we use it to make an already dangerous world safer? The world that large language models entered was very vulnerable. In 2017 NotPetya, a Russian cyber-attack aimed at Ukraine, spread worldwide within hours, deleting data and paralysing systems, including those of Maersk, which carries about a fifth of global shipping. In 2023 Iranian-linked hackers broke into American water utilities, forcing operators to switch to manual control. More worrying still are the attacks not yet triggered: Chinese state

hackers have planted footholds in American power, water and transport systems, ready to strike silently during a conflict. Few of these attacks relied on brilliant new exploits—code that weaponises systems’ flaws to cause harm. They succeeded because the defenders could not find, fix or fence off known weaknesses fast enough across sprawling, ageing systems. The cost of that status quo belongs in any honest accounting of AI risk. I have worked on both sides of cyber-conflict: conducting intelligence operations at America’s National Security Agency, then running its Cybersecurity Directorate to turn those lessons into defences. Later, at the White House, I pushed through the first mandatory cyber-security rules for America’s pipelines, railways and airports. The experience taught me something important: AI can help defenders even more than it can help attackers. True, AI makes the challenge harder in some ways. It lowers the skill needed by attackers, and lets them jiggle every digital door-knob, continuously and at scale. It is already speeding up the discovery of new flaws, including in software foundational to the internet. But the old rule of cyber-conflict, that defenders must be strong everywhere while attackers need to find only one weakness, rested on defenders lacking the time, people and money to cover everything. AI alleviates that problem. AI is already making defenders’ response to these threats stronger. They own their code and can task AI to pre-emptively analyse it line by line from all possible angles of attack, rather than probing it only from the outside as attackers must do. They see their own network traffic, so AI can spot the subtle signs of an intruder using stolen credentials and legitimate tools, as China’s hackers do, which older tools miss. Using AI available today, utilities can turn operational data into virtual replicas of power grids and water systems. These “digital twins” let operators test defences without putting essential services at risk. Attackers, working from outside, can build only crude approximations.
