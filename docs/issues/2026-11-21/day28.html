The incredible shrinking legacy of Donald J. Trump A three-way summit with Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping in China diminished America Nov 19th 2026 IT IS A curious sort of achievement. President Donald Trump has earned himself a giant place in history, while diminishing the office that he holds. That unhappy dynamic was on full display when Mr Trump met Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin on the margins of an APEC leaders’ summit in southern China on November 19th. Mr Trump savoured his starring role, calling it a “big win for us” that Mr Xi met him at the airport, and gave him the seat of honour at an APEC dinner. He was less clear when explaining why the unprecedented three-way meeting might matter. On one hand, he called China and Russia “natural enemies” who, he told Fox News television, had been foolishly driven

together by President Barack Obama. That might suggest that Mr Trump sees a chance to drive a wedge between Mr Putin and Mr Xi, in an echo of the manoeuvre pulled off by Richard Nixon and his envoy, Henry Kissinger, when they met China’s ageing despot, Mao Zedong, in 1972, and worked to split China from the Soviet Union. On the other hand, Mr Trump seemed uninterested in wedge-driving. After declaring that vast, sparsely populated Russia naturally fears crowded, populous China, Mr Trump pivoted to bragging about how he is friends with Mr Putin and Mr Xi, presenting this as a good thing. Mr Trump sounded less like a statesman focused on the global balance of power than a needy high- school student crashing the cool kids’ party. “They both like Trump. Now, what happens after me, that I can’t tell you,” he told Fox News. Stepping back from that Trumpian incoherence, the meeting left America worse off, while enhancing the standing of China and Russia. Harm was done even though the Putin-Trump-Xi meeting lacked real substance. At the time we went to press, it fell far short of being the “new Yalta” described in some breathless headlines: a nod to the conference during the second world war where Europe was divided into American- and Soviet-led blocs. By all accounts, the original idea was Russia’s, as Mr Putin saw a chance to secure support from America and China for a Ukraine settlement on his terms. In essence, the Russian was asking Mr Trump and Mr Xi to help him carve out a European sphere of influence for himself, like a trio of bewhiskered 19th-century statesmen at a map-strewn mahogany table. The nakedness of that Russian pitch appears to have embarrassed China. In late October Mr Xi irritably told the visiting German president, Frank-Walter Steinmeier, that he had no plans to co-host a Ukraine summit with America and Russia, and claimed he did not know where such rumours came from. Of course Europe should be included in any peace talks, Mr Xi added. China is juggling competing interests when it comes to the Ukraine war. On the one hand, China does not want to see Mr Putin defeated by Ukraine and a united West. To that end, China supplies Russian armsmakers with everything from ball bearings to jet engines for attack drones, and keeps Russia’s economy afloat by buying its oil and gas. On the other hand,

Chinese leaders resent how the war has gravely damaged their relations with Europe. For Mr Putin, the meeting was the win. To sip tea with Mr Trump and Mr Xi, even while his missiles are murdering Ukrainian civilians in their homes and railway stations, exposes Western disunity and the failure of attempts to isolate him. Yes, he is China’s dependent junior partner. But he got to hobnob with Earth’s two most powerful men. Mr Xi also gains. The world saw Mr Putin and Mr Trump, two leaders embroiled in disastrous wars in Ukraine and Iran, paying tribute to a China that is at peace. The site was carefully chosen. Shenzhen is a futuristic megacity, home to dancing robots, flying cars and other marvels. Mr Xi called the three powers “major victorious countries” of the second world war, who should now uphold world peace through mutual respect and avoiding one another’s “red lines”. How handy to inhabit a post-pariah world, in which big powers can invade the small with impunity. In sad contrast, Mr Trump looked like a loser. Before the three-way meeting, he called it a chance to discuss “a lot of things”, including the Iran war. In Shenzhen, he downplayed the influence of China and Russia on that conflict, dismissing as “peanuts” the help that they give to Iran. In their meeting he called the three powers “uniquely” placed to tackle nuclear-arms control. Big guys talking and let others sweat the details.” The pursuit of fame drives Mr Trump, as shown by his willingness to meet Mr Putin and Mr Xi on their terms. Their release of ChatGPT four years ago set the artificial-intelligence boom in motion. Before long countless chatbot imitators had emerged, but none has caught up in popularity. By last year OpenAI’s annual revenue had reached $13bn— then nearly triple that of its arch-rival, Anthropic (see chart). And yet it is Anthropic, founded by OpenAI secessionists, that has raised the largest sum of all time through an initial public offering (IPO), with a total haul of $107bn. Before trading began on November 19th, after we went to press, the firm was worth $1.5trn, topping the $1.3trn at which the still unlisted OpenAI was valued in a recent private fundraising round. OpenAI may have led the first phase of competition among model-makers, based around chatbots, but Anthropic trounced it in the second, where

coding agents became the biggest source of business. Usage of Claude Code, Anthropic’s software-writing bot, has rocketed this year. Which strategy will win? Both firms are counting on blistering growth in the years ahead to justify their valuations.
