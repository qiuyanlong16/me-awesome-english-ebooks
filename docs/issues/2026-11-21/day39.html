Raph Koster, a game-industry veteran, estimates that the cost of developing a top-quality game has risen tenfold every decade since the mid-1990s. “GTA VI” reportedly cost over $1bn—and it shows in every detail, from the swish of Lucia’s ponytail as she flees across rooftops, to the wake of Jason’s speedboat as he chases a seaplane. One developer has compared making the game to building “a pyramid on Everest”. (As budgets have swollen larger than Hollywood’s, apparently so have egos.) Companies are responding by making fewer, bigger bets. Xbox, Microsoft’s gaming division, has jettisoned some of its smaller studios to focus on reliable performers like “Call of Duty” and “Minecraft”. Rather than gamble on new titles, publishers are turning existing hits into constantly evolving “live service” games, notes Niko Partners, a research firm. Whereas “GTA IV” (2008) was mainly a single-player game, selling 25m copies in five years, “GTA V” (2013) has become an open-ended online experience, selling 230m copies over more than a decade. Despite its age, the franchise made more than $800m last year. Even for companies with deep pockets, breaking into gaming’s top tier has become tough. In 2024 Sony launched “Concord”, a live-service shooting game with a reported nine-figure budget—only to shut it down after two weeks, amid weak demand. Earlier this month Sony cancelled another shooter, “Fairgames”, after lukewarm feedback. Players have invested so much time and money in titles such as “GTA” and “Fortnite”—honing expertise and buying in-game items—that moving to another game represents a steep cost. The top live-service titles have earned the nickname “black-hole games”, from which players cannot be dragged away. Some believe that artificial intelligence could shake up the market by allowing developers to generate code, graphics, music and so on more quickly and cheaply. Tim Sweeney, head of Epic Games, which makes “Fortnite”, has said he expects AI to bring forth “a new golden age of gaming”.

Yet even if AI reduces the cost of making software, it is pushing up the price of hardware. Soaring demand from data centres for computer memory has caused a cost surge dubbed the “RAMageddon”. Earlier this year PlayStation and Xbox raised their prices; both consoles now cost more than when they launched six years ago. Analysts believe the next generation of consoles may cost over $1,000. Xbox is laying off thousands of workers. “The future of Xbox itself is now an open question,” argue Gareth Sutcliffe and colleagues at Enders Analysis, a research firm. All this could mean that the future of gaming looks more like the past. In a world of $1,000 consoles, consumers will shift to lower-spec devices, such as cheap PCs and mobiles, says Mr Piscatella, who notes that games like “Fortnite” and “Minecraft” have been designed to run “on a toaster”. Nintendo has struck gold with its Switch console, which lacks the computing muscle to run games like “GTA VI” but has been a hit thanks to its portability and low price. Youngsters are gravitating towards Roblox, a free platform featuring millions of basic games made by users. AI is making it easier for tiny development teams to turn out cheap and cheerful experiences for the platform. Roblox recently said that two games in its top 20, “Break and Steal an Egg” and “Huss Valley”, had been created “almost entirely” with AI.

To hardcore gamers, such titles will feel like a step back. Most of the top games on Roblox look like titles from 20 years ago. Nintendo’s latest Switch has the computing power of a console from the 2010s. Yet as the economics of launching new top-tier games get harder, and audiences shift to lower- powered devices, these will be the games that most people play. The decade- long gestation and billion-dollar budget behind “GTA VI” have created a one-of-a-kind piece of entertainment. As one in-game character reflects: “Enjoy this, dude. In 1952 an undistinguished journalist (and birding enthusiast) called Ian Fleming sat down at his desk to write “the spy story to end all spy stories”. Fleming knew some things about his book: he knew he wanted his hero to be manly, his heroines to be comely —and his income to be vast. But what he did not know was the name of his spy. Then he caught sight of a book on the shelf above his desk. Its title—“Field Guide to Birds of the West Indies”—was dull. Its pages, which contained a lot about the Pied-billed Grebe, were duller still. Its author’s name was dullest of all. Fleming thought it was “brief, unromantic,

Anglo-Saxon and yet very masculine”. The name, as Fleming would write on page 56 of his new book, was “Bond—James Bond”. Names, for Bond, matter. On November 16th, one more name was announced: that of the actor who will play the British secret agent in the 26th official Bond film. This new name is, like the original, unromantic and masculine: it is Robert Pattinson. Mr Pattinson is in some ways a surprising choice. The actors chosen to play Bond are usually fairly unknown. Mr Pattinson, however, has been a box- office draw since 2008, when he starred as a sultry vampire in the “Twilight” fantasy films; this year alone he has roles in four major movies. Purists may fear that he will suck the life out of the franchise, and perhaps even switch from martinis to bloody Marys. Yet he perfectly fits the physical description: he is tall, dark and square- jawed. He has a dash of both the hero (in “Harry Potter” he played a gallant prefect) and villain (as he showed in this year’s adaptation of “The Odyssey”) alike, which is good for Bond, who is both. Though he is, at his best, more subtle than Bond, who is a bit of a lunk: Fleming called his character a “cardboard booby”. Mr Pattinson matters because Bond does. The Bond franchise is huge: it is made up of 14 original books, dozens of continuation books and 25 films which have grossed more than $23bn (after adjusting for inflation). It is thought that half the world has seen at least one Bond film. For Britain he matters psychologically, too. Now, just as when the books were written, Britain feels like an international has-been. Bond offers succour in spy form. Britain’s army may be puny; its economy shaky—but it still has a man who knows how to get the baddie (and mix a drink). His casting is treated as something of national import, as if one were casting not Bond but Britannia. The choice feels particularly loaded now that Amazon MGM, an American studio, has taken creative control of the franchise. Every literary character brought to the screen belongs to two eras: the one that produced the book and the one that produces the film. The art is to serve both while betraying neither. Adaptations that succeed—think of Andrew

Davies’s version of “Pride and Prejudice” in 1995—do. Mr Davies left Jane Austen’s dialogue all but unchanged, but added some 20th-century smoulder. The stage directions for the lake scene read “Darcy has an erection.” Not an original Austen line. Bond is harder still to remake, for he belongs to three eras. The third is the wartime age that produced Fleming himself. Fleming based his characters on the “tough commando types” he met in British naval intelligence: men who had an excess of charm, derring-do and hyphens. Bond is said to have been inspired by men like Conrad O’Brien-ffrench (a spy who skied beautifully and wore a moustache dashingly) and an officer named Patrick Dalzel-Job (who parachuted skilfully and accepted the surrender of Bremen personally). It was a beguiling age of heroes and eccentrics. “M”, the fictional head of MI6, Britain’s foreign intelligence service, is thought to have been inspired by Maxwell Knight. As Ben Macintyre notes in “For Your Eyes Only”, a history of Fleming and Bond, the spymaster kept snakes in his bath and wrote a book called “How to Keep a Gorilla” (presumably not in the bath with the snakes). One of the founding heads of Britain’s Secret Service Bureau, the precursor to MI6, was a man who said his male hires should be able to “make notes on their shirt cuff while riding on horseback”. Female ones, meanwhile, should have “good legs”. Bond is a little of all of these figures—and of Fleming himself, who was a notorious womaniser, and a perfunctory one. As one former girlfriend put it, women were like “fishcakes” for him: he was “very fond of fishcakes, but he never pretended there was any mystique about eating them”. Bond duly consumes a lot of women, often in a tasteless fashion. Fleming described his books as “the pillow fantasies of an adolescent mind”. The films were often little better. One featured a character called Christmas Jones, setting up a smutty double entendre about how often Christmas “comes”. Fleming felt Bond looked a little like him too, though “more handsome”.
