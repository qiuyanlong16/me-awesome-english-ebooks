Education must adapt to how the young now get their news Schools should help them figure out how to disagree more constructively than their elders, writes Sam Freedman Nov 19th 2026 MORE THAN half of the world’s 18- to 24-year-olds now say that social media and chatbots are their main way to access news. For younger teenagers that number is even higher. Ofcom, Britain’s communications regulator, found that 78% of the country’s 12- to 15-year-olds get their news this way. Adults, too, are shifting their habits, but more slowly. For young people the change is profound. Previously teenagers had to actively seek out news; now it finds them. Even in the past few years things have shifted as social media has moved from participatory networks to algorithmic discovery. A lot of news is inserted into feeds without being sought.

This naturally leads to concern about misinformation, and it is an issue. Algorithms reinforce opinions or, worse, push views held by company owners. A recent study, published in Nature, randomised American users of Elon Musk’s X so that half saw the algorithmic “for you” feed and half a chronological one of people they followed. The first group were 5.5% likelier to view investigations into Donald Trump as unacceptable. But news consumers have always been subject to biases. A pensioner reading a partisan newspaper every day is also being fed misinformation, albeit by a human editor rather than an algorithm. If anything, young people are savvier than older ones about the reliability of online content. The bigger issue is that children are seeing a lot more news than they used to and it is unfiltered. Videos on all sorts of topics flash by in a muddle without context or explanation. This content also increasingly diverges from what older adults might be seeing on the BBC, or on their more sedate social-media feeds. It is, for instance, typically much less focused on domestic news, with a strong bias towards America, where many content creators live. Your average 16-year- old in Britain is more likely to understand a Zohran Mamdani meme than to know that John Healey is the British chancellor. For teachers around the world this is a new and difficult challenge. They are used to helping students gradually build up a picture of the world through the controlled delivery of knowledge. Now they are confronted with pupils drinking from a firehose of information and struggling to contextualise any of it. I am currently chairing a commission for The Economist Educational Foundation with the aim of supporting schools to manage this new world. We have done a lot of polling and focus groups with young people and teachers in Britain, and there is no doubt that it is a problem. At one school, a group of 11-year-old girls told us the big news story in their feeds that week was a contentious case of alleged rape at Cornell, an American university, something that did not feature much in the mainstream British media. The boys in the class had not heard about it—an indication of the kind of information divides that algorithms can create.

Our polling found teachers are worried about how to deal with this. One challenge is simply a lack of resources. Another is a lack of confidence in discussing controversial and divisive issues such as Israel’s occupation of Gaza or race riots. One head teacher, in an area where a lot of St George’s flags were put up on lampposts in the summer of 2025, talked about how difficult it was to discuss this issue with concerned students when parents of other children had been involved in putting the flags up. When schools do venture into current affairs they tend to avoid these areas of controversy, but it is important to be able to talk about them—not least to help young people work out how to disagree in more constructive ways than their elders. This is something social media actively discourages by incentivising polarising language. Another study of X found that highly negative posts about political opponents significantly outperform positive ones about allies. Increasingly, people say they are less likely to be friends with people who hold a different view. Counteracting this in a classroom, while dealing with sensitive issues, is a skill that requires training—something The Economist Educational Foundation is working on. At the moment learning how to manage these kinds of conversation, which increasingly occur spontaneously in lessons, is not a formal part of teacher-training courses. It also requires time. The new curriculum in England, due to be taught from September 2028, gives more importance to citizenship and will make it a compulsory subject at primary school, which will help. But conversations also need to be woven into other relevant subjects. One uncertainty is the impact of social-media bans for children. Britain’s ban, on use by under-16s, is set to come in next year. This will limit access, but it might worsen the information environment as some will find ways of gaining access while others don’t. Scraps of footage being shared on WhatsApp groups will have even less context. Either way there will be no return to the old model. Teenagers aren’t suddenly going to start reading physical newspapers or watching the evening news. A core purpose of education is to give young people the knowledge they need to be active citizens. It is no longer possible to do that without tackling the very different ways they now see the world. ■

Sam Freedman is a senior fellow at the Institute for Government, a senior adviser to Ark Schools and chair of the Current Affairs Education Commission.
