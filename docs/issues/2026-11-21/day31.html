Just how central should one person be to an organisation? From founder mode to bounder mode Nov 19th 2026 IT has not been a great few days for the idea of “founder mode”. The term was coined by Paul Graham, an essayist and venture capitalist, but the concept came from Brian Chesky, the founder of Airbnb. Mr Chesky gave a speech in 2024 laying out his belief that founders mistakenly delegate too much authority to managers below them as their firms grow bigger. He proposed a much more hands-on approach in which they dive relentlessly into the details, set the tempo of the company and make it clear where everyone should be focused. Instead of silos, a shared sense of purpose; instead of a leader insulated from the work, someone steeped in it. Until a few days ago, this argument was strengthened by the example of Jensen Huang. The erstwhile boss of Nvidia had presided over an

extraordinary success story, and had his tentacles everywhere. He flattened the organisation: around 60 people reported to him directly. He hoovered up information, by encouraging everyone in the chipmaker to send him emails about what was on their minds. He eschewed one-to-one meetings, arguing that it was better for lots of people to hear what he thought. He, and his wretched leather jacket, embodied the firm. How could one person manage to be across so much, he was sometimes asked. I do nothing but work, he would say. Well, not exactly. Mr Huang’s energy levels were even more impressive than people imagined. His abrupt downfall after an affair with an alleged Chinese agent, and subsequent campaigning for reinstatement, raise questions about whether one person should ever be so essential to a big organisation. Whether founder mode makes sense depends partly on how far the idea is taken. An all-in bet on the sustained genius of a single individual is always going to entail risks. But some of the precepts of founder mode are benign. In a new bonus episode of “Boss Class”, The Economist’s podcast on management, recorded before Mr Huang’s resignation, Mr Chesky describes his role at Airbnb as being akin to an editor’s. He reviews much of the work at Airbnb himself, using meetings with people several levels down the firm. Whether a chief executive is a founder or a salaried manager, there is a lot to be said for this “skip-level” approach. Employees usually respond well to the chance to spend time with the boss. People waste ludicrous amounts of time second-guessing CEOs; it is often more efficient just to hear from them directly. And it’s good for bosses to see the quality of the people down the organisation.
