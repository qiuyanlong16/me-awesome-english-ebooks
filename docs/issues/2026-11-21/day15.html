Donald Trump’s love of “conquest” is more self- defeating than ever Yet Marco Rubio and others continue to celebrate it Nov 19th 2026 Among Roman generals, Pompey was a prodigy, if something of a rake. (Plutarch records that “Flora the courtesan” loved to recount that she never left his embraces “without bearing the marks of his teeth”.) He was in his early 20s when he was dispatched to Sicily in 82BC to suppress opponents of a Roman leader. He treated them all with kindness, Plutarch writes, except one group that sought safety in Roman law from the soldiers. Pompey mocked them. He executed two Roman citizens after treating them with “unnatural” cruelty, violating ancient custom by putting at least one to death without a trial. Pompey’s contempt for the law sped the cycle of violence that ultimately put a bloody end to his own life, and Julius Caesar’s, and the republic itself. So

it seemed strange when, on November 13th, the secretary of state, Marco Rubio, a lawyer, told a room full of lawyers that the Trump administration’s response to anyone invoking international law would probably be the same as Pompey’s: “He said, ‘Stop quoting laws to men with swords.’” Stranger still, in speaking to the conservative Federalist Society, Mr Rubio drew a parallel between the International Criminal Court (ICC) and “activist courts” at home. Both were “alien to who we are”. And who is that? Americans, he said, have always shared “a hunger to expand, to grow, to explore, to conquer”. Is it possible, at least, to be shocked that in less than two years such extremism from an American administration has lost its capacity to shock? That America’s chief diplomat would celebrate threats of violence rather than respect for law, and conquest as an American virtue, may be as unsurprising as discovering that Donald Trump and his men with swords are not chastened by the midterm elections. Still, the administration’s Pompeian contempt seems more self-defeating than ever, politically and strategically. Last month a Gallup poll found Americans trusted Democrats to protect them from military threats more than Republicans for the first time since 2007. Three years ago, Republicans led by 22 points. Moreover, Mr Trump, as he jetted off to a summit in Asia, hoped to negotiate a nuclear accord with China and Russia. Why would any foreign power sign an agreement with Americans who believe rules are for sissies or suckers? Mr Trump has a pattern of tearing up not only agreements reached by past administrations, but those he signed himself. Such scorn for international rules is particularly dangerous when a new weapon, artificial intelligence, is loose in the world, and becoming more potent by the day. It is possible, of course, that Mr Rubio, who trimmed his internationalism to fit Mr Trump’s America-first agenda, does not mean everything he says. American administrations have always interpreted international law with a generous eye to the national interest. But past administrations identified that interest with supporting the rules-based order that America championed, after two world wars, to provide global stability. Mr Trump regards such rules as not protecting sovereignty but assaulting it. “To the victor belong the spoils,” he informed the UN General Assembly in September, referring to the oil rights he claimed after invading Venezuela to capture its president, Nicolás Maduro.
