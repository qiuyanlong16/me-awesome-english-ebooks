United States | The judge who thought differently

United States

Mr Posner came to prominence in the 1970s as part of a group of scholars who sought to use the tools of economics to study law. Surveying centuries of decisions in areas such as contracts and criminal law, Mr Posner argued that judges had developed doctrines that approximated economically efficient outcomes. He went on to venture far beyond law and economics, writing more than 500 articles, 50 books and 3,300 judicial opinions on everything from the law and sex to how judges think. A 2021 study in the University of Chicago Law Review ranked him as the most cited American legal scholar of all time, comfortably ahead of his nearest rival. Compared with the left-leaning legal academy of the 1960s and 1970s, he was conservative. That helps explain why in 1981 Ronald Reagan, then president, nominated Mr Posner to the Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals. But although he was market-oriented, he was not motivated by ideology or arcane legal debates. Take an opinion he wrote in 2014, striking down bans on gay marriage in Wisconsin and Indiana, the year before the Supreme Court did the same. Mr Posner argued that the bans should be judged simply on whether their benefits outweighed their harm to gay couples and their children. He concluded that they did not. He had previously opposed the idea of a constitutional right to gay marriage. But despite his forceful style, he was willing to admit being wrong. “Law is not a science,” he wrote, explaining his change of mind. “The notion that the 21st century can be ruled by documents authored in the 18th and mid-19th centuries is nonsense.” In the last decades of his career, such views put him out of step with the conservative legal world’s love affair with originalism, a philosophy that attempts to decide cases based on the presumed intentions of the law’s authors. He was particularly critical of its greatest exponent on the Supreme Court, Antonin Scalia. In a review of the justice’s book, he criticised originalists for denying their political motivations and hiding behind semantics. Mr Posner’s judicial doctrine was simpler, if less predictable. In 2016 he wrote that it came from a favourite poet, William Butler Yeats: “And I grew weary of the sun / Until my thoughts cleared up again, / Remembering that the best I have done / Was done to make it plain.” His decisions fill law-

school casebooks, not least because they are so easy to read. “His writing was always clear and careful. It was elegant, but didn’t go for the clever quip,” says Guido Calabresi, a federal judge. His willingness to be frank may be why he was never nominated to the Supreme Court. The contrast between his style and the justices’ was made clear during a 2019 oral argument. The case at hand concerned whether Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 covered discrimination based on sexual orientation in addition to gender. Mr Posner had concluded that it did for practical reasons: deciding otherwise would put “the entire burden of updating old statutes on the legislative branch”. The court reached the same result, but through more convoluted means. A layered fight over a marginal commodity Free-market purists want to legalise onion-futures trading Nov 19th 2026 It was a financial scandal to make you cry. In the autumn of 1955, Vincent Kosuga and Sam Siegel set out to make a fortune from onions. The two men bought onions futures on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) and began hoarding vast quantities of the vegetable in warehouses in and around the city. The pair told a group of farmers that they would flood the market unless the farmers bought some of their onions at a healthy price. Meanwhile, the duo quietly accumulated a giant short position on the futures market. In early 1956, when the onions were soon to rot, they sold them en masse. Kosuga and Siegel made out like bandits. Two years later future President Gerald Ford, then a congressman representing an onion-rich region of Michigan, sponsored the Onion Futures Act of 1958, to ban the trading of onion-futures contracts. Farmers had no

way to hedge against price swings. The CME suddenly had a fifth of its business abolished—it turned to trading contracts for other goods, from pork bellies to frozen orange juice concentrate. Now, however, an improbable campaign is trying to overturn the ban—an effort led by (who else) undergraduates at the University of Chicago, a temple of free-market economics. Their demonstrations feature adapted protest songs with titles such as “Which Side Are You Onion?”, and are perhaps the first-ever gatherings where someone shouts “We love free markets” through a megaphone. A recent event at the university was even attended by two women, one holding a sign that read “Hot girls hedge onions”. The students’ complaint is simple. Siegel and Kosuga were able to hoard such a large share of onions in the 1950s because the market was smaller and much of the vegetables’ supply moved through Chicago anyway, by rail. They also built up a short position in secret. Now onions travel around the country in a more dispersed manner, by truck, and traders must report their positions. That makes the ban pointless, argues Adam Ash, a third-year economics student who led the singing. In 2026, being able to trade futures would help farmers hedge volatility, rather than expose them to shady financiers. Mr Ash acknowledges that onions are not a pressing problem, but insists the students’ effort is entirely serious. “We care about it because it is small,” he says. If America’s democracy can’t change a silly law from the 1950s, what can it do? They have caught the attention of at least one congressman, Sean Casten, who represents some of Chicago’s southern suburbs.
