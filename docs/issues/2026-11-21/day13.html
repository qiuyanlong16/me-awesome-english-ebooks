The return of measles in America Reversing an absurd, unnecessary failure will be expensive and slow Nov 19th 2026 The failure is as monumental as it was predictable. For years, misinformation had helped depress America’s vaccination rates. The covid- 19 pandemic made things worse by disrupting routine vaccinations and deepening distrust of jabs. At the start of his second term, Donald Trump appointed Robert Kennedy junior, a vaccine sceptic, as the country’s top health official. Mr Kennedy’s contributions since then have included urging West Virginia to offer religious exemptions to schools’ vaccine requirements and an executive order encouraging drugmakers to split the combined jab protecting against measles, mumps and rubella (MMR). Mr Trump claimed, with no evidence, that together “there could be a possibility they’re quite lethal.”

So it was little surprise when, on November 17th, an arm of the World Health Organisation declared that endemic transmission of measles has been re-established in America for the first time since 1999. It is nonetheless a stunning step backward for the country. This year has seen cases reported in 45 states and 11 measles-related deaths, compared with just three from 2000 to 2025. Fighting measles now will be costly and painful, for the Americans sickened by it and the health system once able to fend it off. Measles is highly contagious, an airborne pathogen that takes up residence in a patient’s nose and throat before symptoms appear. In most cases those symptoms include a fever and rash. But among children under five who contract measles, one in six are hospitalised with complications such as brain inflammation or pneumonia; the rate for adults is almost as high. Even mild cases cast a long shadow. Measles causes “immune amnesia”, a loss of antibodies developed against common viruses, which can lead to more frequent illness for several years. One in 600 children infected before their first birthday will eventually develop a fatal brain condition. After a vaccine was developed in the 1960s, vaccination efforts proceeded fitfully until the early 1990s, when Congress authorised free jabs for children. By the end of the century measles was no longer spreading and occasional outbreaks were easily contained.

That era is over. Two-thirds of counties in Pennsylvania and nearly a third of counties in Wisconsin have reported measles cases in 2026. Transmission in Utah and Arizona have been traced to an outbreak in Texas that began over a year ago. Stopping this will be harder than it was the first time around, says Joseph Kanter of the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials, a non- profit group in Virginia. In the 1990s vaccines could focus on children, because most adults had either contracted the disease in childhood or been vaccinated in a previous drive. Now adults account for about 40% of cases. Muddled health policy will make the task more difficult, too. Since August the White House has directed an additional $100m to fight measles and vaccine-preventable diseases, but that may not be enough. To contain an outbreak, public-health workers must trace the contacts of infected people, ask them to isolate if they are not immune, and organise vaccination drives, often going door-to-door to make the case for the jab. That makes containment costly—an average of $43,000 per measles case—and complicated, even when the health system is trusted and fully staffed. It isn’t. The Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has lost 30% of its staff since March 2025, according to the American Federation of Government Employees, a federal workers’ union, so is less equipped to help local efforts. “Everybody is trying to pick up the pieces” of what the CDC normally does, says Jeanne Marrazzo of the Infectious Diseases Society of America, a group of doctors and scientists. State health departments are issuing their own guidelines on treating measles, as most doctors have never seen a child with the disease, let alone an adult. Professional societies are running webinars featuring doctors from outbreak hotspots, a practice that emerged in the early days of covid-19. On November 17th Mr Kennedy posted on X that he recommended MMR vaccination and that America would “work to regain U.S. measles elimination status”. The country may eventually succeed.
