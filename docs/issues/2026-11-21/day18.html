Middle East & Africa | Lost treasures The hunt for Sudan’s looted heritage Priceless antiquities stolen in the war could take decades to recover Nov 19th 2026 Two Nubian kings stand watch over a fire-scorched courtyard, empty but for the lions carved from sandstone guarding the entrance. When Rihab Khider, a Sudanese archaeologist, returned to Sudan’s national museum last year, the first time since the civil war began in 2023, almost everything she found had been damaged or destroyed. Ancient artefacts, some thousands of years old, lay strewn across the overgrown garden and on the busy road outside. “It was a very hard time for me,” she recalls. All she could do was to start picking up the pieces. More than a year later, the museum in Khartoum, Sudan’s capital, remains a shell. Only a few artefacts survive (pictured on next page). The cabinets inside the main exhibition hall have nothing to display. For nearly two years the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), the paramilitary group fighting Sudan’s

army, occupied the city, ruining large swathes of it. Tens of thousands of people were killed. Like many other buildings, the museum was turned into a military encampment. By the time the RSF was driven out in 2025, an estimated 4,000 or more of the museum’s most precious possessions had vanished; even mummies, embalmed as long ago as 2,500BC, were stolen. The plunder recalled the looting of heritage in Syria and Iraq by Islamic State. Recovering the loot could take decades. Much may be lost for ever. Before the war the national museum housed more than 100,000 artefacts, including 2,000 gold objects from the Kingdom of Kush, an ancient civilisation that at its height ruled swathes of Sudan and Egypt. These had been stored in a secure room; the RSF blew the door open and made off with the lot. Accounting for other items is harder. The museum had no complete inventory before the war. Of the documentation that did exist, much has been damaged or lost. The national museum is not the only one that has suffered. The country has eight provincial museums, including three in Darfur, a vast region in the west largely controlled by the RSF, where some of the most important artefacts were held. “The entire timeline of Sudan was in the regional museums,” says Ali Nour of Blue Shield Sudan, a heritage-protection body established in 2024. Many are thought to have been ransacked. Yet sending teams to inspect them would be perilous. The curator of the museum in el- Geneina, in west Darfur, was killed inside the building along with his family. The curators of the museums in el-Fasher and Nyala are missing. Some antiquities have found their way home. Sudanese police recovered an undamaged gilded statue of an archer, a masterpiece of Meroitic art dating from the second or third century BC, in Khartoum. In January the authorities announced the return of 570 stolen objects, including funerary statues of Kushite kings, apparently recovered elsewhere in the country. But much of the loot is likely to have crossed Sudan’s borders, into Ethiopia, South Sudan and Chad. Ikhlass Abdelatif, who chairs Sudan’s antiquities- repatriation committee, says the RSF contacted the museum earlier this year hoping to sell back some of the artefacts, but then fell silent. She suspects that much of the plunder has ended up in the United Arab Emirates, an

important ally of the RSF and a hub for laundering antiquities stolen across the Middle East and north Africa. To hasten their recovery, Sudan’s government has offered rewards for information about the objects’ whereabouts and issued a global alert via Interpol. A government unit that tries to stymie the trafficking of cultural property is working with the British Museum and other international institutions to help police and officials in other countries know how to identify Sudanese artefacts. A team of archaeologists is racing to build a comprehensive inventory and put it online. It will not be easy. No object stolen during the war has yet surfaced on the international art market, be it in auction houses or online marketplaces such as eBay. “With museum thefts we are often talking about decades” for objects to show up, says Olivia Whitting of the Art Loss Register, a London- based art-recovery firm. And Sudan is not yet a party to the UN convention against the illicit trade in cultural property, making it harder to secure the return of those that do. For now, the guardians of Sudan’s heritage are focused on preserving what has not been stolen. Nearly four years in, Sudan’s war shows no sign of letting up; today even cultural sites far from the battlefield can be struck by drones. “Our priority is to protect what we already have,” says Mahmoud Suleiman, boss of Sudan’s National Corporation for Antiquities and Museums. Middle East & Africa | No one is missing the missing imam Iran is functioning without a supreme leader Six men from a council of generals and bureaucrats now dominate decision making Nov 19th 2026 “Yeki bood, yeki nabood,” Persian stories begin: “There was one, and there was no one.” Just such a fairy tale has taken over political life in Iran. For four decades there was a micromanaging supreme leader. Now there is no one. Mojtaba Khamenei, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei’s son, is said to have survived the Israeli strike that killed much of his family on the first day of the war. He formally rules in his father’s place. But he has neither been seen nor heard since. Iranians are discovering life without a meddlesome cleric at the helm. Until the Islamic revolution in 1979, Shiism had been led by a hidden imam for more than a millennium. Today, Iranian officials and Western intelligence agencies insist Mojtaba remains alive. He is said to remain

underground because of a reasonable fear of assassination. Some regime hands say he is controlling affairs behind the scenes, but his silence is deafening. Even if he were active in politics, Mojtaba would need time to consolidate power, given lingering opposition to dynastic succession and his limited religious credentials. In his absence the Islamic Republic’s clerical imprint has grown visibly lighter. Iranians returning from abroad since the war include a pop star sentenced in absentia for incorporating Koranic verses into his songs. Even the daughters of some officials uncover their hair. Some smarter restaurants in Tehran now serve French wine. The most important shift is in how the regime wields power. “What’s principally changed is that there’s no clear, final arbiter,” says Sanam Vakil of Chatham House, a think-tank in London. Appointments to the state broadcaster, government ministries and the judiciary were once subject to approval by the supreme leader’s 44,000-man court; today the executive has a freer hand. “Israel did Iranians a favour getting rid of Khamenei,” quipped an Iranian academic recently in Tehran. “You don’t need to wait any longer for his legitimation [or approval]. It’s more efficient.” Instead, six men, all from the ranks of the Supreme National Security Council, a 13-man body of generals and bureaucrats, dominate decision- making. They are believed to include Masoud Pezeshkian, the president; Major General Ahmad Vahidi, commander-in-chief of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), Iran’s principal military force; Mohsen Rezaei, the council’s secretary and a former IRGC chief; Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf, the speaker of parliament and a former IRGC brigadier general; Ali Abdollahi, the chief of the general staff of the armed forces; and Abbas Araghchi, the foreign minister, the only other civilian among them. The biggest decisions require the collective’s consent. These include matters such as talks about ending or restarting the war with America or nuclear policy. But they each also have a degree of autonomy. Mr Rezaei has been a stalwart of the system since becoming IRGC chief during the Iran-Iraq war in the 1980s. Mr Vahidi keeps order in the ranks of the IRGC; and Mr Qalibaf runs parliament’s affairs.

Mr Pezeshkian has largely been left to manage a war-induced depression and a currency in near free fall. The economy was down by 10% in the second quarter of this year compared with the same period in 2025. Unemployment has swelled by half a million since the war began, even as the labour force shrinks and over half of all jobs are now informal. And yet Mr Pezeshkian seems to be managing the fallout from war better than many had expected. He has kept the lights on, despite oil refineries and power plants being bombed. Mr Pezeshkian has tried to open Iran’s borders to trade since the war began. Overland trade has increased. Some tankers are reportedly paying the tolls Iran demands for passage via the Strait of Hormuz. But Iran is struggling to evade America’s blockade on the export of oil from the southern ports. America’s armada cannot hermetically seal all 2,800km of Iran’s coastline, but estimates of exports in September averaged 260,000 barrels per day, or only around one-sixth of the pre-war flow. Not all senior officials neatly fit under the six-man committee’s command, however. Domestic control remains largely in the hands of Hossein Taeb, an IRGC hard-man, former intelligence chief and clerical ideologue. He is said to have strong-armed clerics into electing Mojtaba, a longtime confidant, and has long been disliked by some within the establishment for his enthusiasm for a hereditary dynasty. His current role as head of the Basij, a vast paramilitary force, gives him a powerful base. Since the war began his henchmen and -women have staged nightly rallies in squares across Iran, where anti-regime protesters might otherwise gather. They have distributed rifles to loyalists. In a further effort to intimidate would-be dissidents, Mr Taeb has overseen nearly 700 executions this year. In January the regime crushed nationwide demonstrations with massacres. The war has also helped suppress unrest. Patriotic sentiment has grown as the country’s infrastructure came under American and Israeli attack. Domestic unrest has slipped. On questions of war and peace, the six-man council has set aside ideological differences. Where the supreme leader once dithered, it moves decisively.
