He was amazed, too, by how terrified he felt the moment things went wrong. Yet he was not afraid of speaking out. For what horrified him most was the total denial of the programme by the Putin regime, from Vladimir Vladimirovich down. They said they had never heard of Novichok, or the programme’s formal codename “Foliant”. Obviously the British secret services had staged Salisbury themselves, and then tried to pin it on Russia. True, the chances of tracing the agent’s origin were close to zero. But to deny all knowledge of it was to wipe out his whole career, from university in Moscow to the most hidden research arms of the Soviet Defence Ministry. He disappeared into thin air. After the Skripal case he therefore made himself fully available to Western journalists and investigations. He tirelessly explained what Novichok was and how it worked, concealing only details of its making that seemed too dangerous to spread. As for the Russian state’s denials, they were a load of shit and total nonsense. He had been slowly disclosing his work since the Soviet Union fell, not least to warn the new satellite states to destroy any Novichok stored with them. Dissenting came easily. In the 1940s his parents had been sent as punishment to the dismal mining town of Kirovsk beyond the Arctic Circle, which gulag prisoners had been forced to build themselves. That was where he grew up. He had no love either for Russia’s government or for the passivity of most Russians towards their police state. But nothing would change. Cheka, NKVD, KGB, FSB: different names, same goons. In August 2020 a man on his wavelength, the opposition leader Alexei Navalny, fell violently ill on a flight from Siberia to Moscow. Drink or drugs, said the Kremlin; Novichok, said the West. Dr Uglev instinctively ruled it out, but lay awake night after night worrying. Navalny had slipped into a coma; A-234 didn’t do that. Others around him would have been affected; they weren’t. Yet many good scientists were convinced Novichok was the culprit. It might well have been A-242, even his own batch. If that were to kill Navalny, it would be hard to bear. But it would confirm again

that Russia not only had had a cold-war Novichok programme but still, despite pledges to destroy it, a stockpile of the stuff. Navalny, as it happened, did not die then. He was killed in prison, the world concluded, in 2024, with epibatidine from South American dart-frogs. Mr Uglev’s own death raised suspicions, too. It happened after a heart attack, his son said. But it was not announced for days.
