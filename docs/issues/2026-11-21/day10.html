China’s efforts to strengthen its safety net fuel public anger People don’t want to pay into an unequal system they don’t trust Nov 19th 2026 Pan Kexin is a 25-year-old barista in the central city of Wuhan. Every month she reluctantly pays one-sixth of her 6,000 yuan ($900) salary into the state’s social-security scheme, which covers five types of insurance: old-age (pension), medical, unemployment, work injury and maternity. “Young people today don’t have much faith in the credibility of these institutions,” Ms Pan reckons. Her friends are mostly gig workers who have not enrolled. “The way they’re now forcing people to pay for social security basically means the government is running out of money,” she says. For many people, China’s social-security system is meagre at best. The government wants to improve it, but is facing huge challenges. Businesses routinely skimp on their contributions by underreporting, or failing to report,

employees’ salaries. People in informal work often make none. The population is ageing rapidly, which places an ever greater burden on the scheme: by the end of 2025 there were more than 320m aged 60 or over, or 23% of the population—up from 16% a decade earlier. And there are fewer people available to make payments: the working-age population fell by 60m in the same period. In the past decade the government has tried to cope by more than doubling fiscal subsidies for social security. But it has also tried to curb the cost of benefits by slowing their annual rise, while pursuing potential contributors more aggressively. Workers and employers alike are grumbling. Ms Pan is formally employed, so she has to contribute. Employees pay at least 10% of their salary, while employers have to hand over the equivalent of about 25% of each worker’s pay cheque. But many people in smaller firms collude with their bosses to evade payments. An employer will not register with the scheme, or underdeclare salaries to reduce both the firm’s social-security bill and deductions from employees’ wages. The difference is paid to employees as cash under the table. The government is getting impatient with foot-draggers. It wants a stronger social-security system in part to revitalise the economy. Consumer spending is weak because people are saving a lot for medical emergencies and retirement: a better safety net would encourage them to open their wallets and thereby stimulate growth. On October 11th the government vowed to use “every possible means” to ensure that firms fully report the pay of all staff and submit the correct contributions. Since November 1st it has imposed standard fines (up to three times the amount owed) on violators. These moves have stirred anger online. On social media many complain their wages are so low that higher payments would be unaffordable. They express doubt that the government will fulfil its pension obligations. Hashtags such as “young people don’t want to pay social security” have spread: censors have blocked some of them. Bosses are squirming, too. In many industries overcapacity is forcing firms to make savings so they can fight price wars. Stricter enforcement of social-security rules could push up their costs, or prompt them to lay off formal employees and turn to gig workers instead.

Even if the government succeeds in boosting compliance by firms, it will struggle among gig workers. By some estimates there are about 320m of them, double the number in 2019. For gig workers enrolment is voluntary; but those who do sign up have to pay all their contributions themselves. Some delivery firms have offered to shoulder part of that cost, but few workers have opted in. A survey of delivery drivers in one district of Beijing found that 40% preferred extra payments in cash. The far better deals enjoyed by people on the state’s payroll have deepened resentment of the government’s pressure. In September a netizen’s back-of- the-envelope calculation of payments received by Wuhan University’s retired staff set off a national discussion about stark inequality in welfare benefits. Urban public-sector workers get far more generous pensions than most people in China. Older workers tend to put more trust in the system. Ms Pan’s parents, for example, are self-employed, so are not obliged to enroll. But they do make payments, believing they are worthwhile. Young Chinese are the biggest grumblers. They fume online about funding the generous packages of retired civil servants, when they themselves have a hard time finding jobs: youth unemployment is nearly 20%. The authorities have been fighting back. People’s Daily, the Communist Party’s main mouthpiece, recently attacked those who “question the fairness and sustainability” of the government’s pension provisions. It called for “vigilance” against those “propagating narratives such as ‘the system is on the verge of collapse’ or ‘young people can no longer afford to support the elderly.’” Resentment will grow unless more people see rewards from compliance. “If policymakers really wanted to prioritise boosting consumption, then raising the level of benefits would be more effective in the near term than broadening coverage and enforcing contributions,” wrote Gavekal Dragonomics, a research firm, in a recent bulletin. But the party is hesitant. “We must resolutely avoid falling into the trap of welfarism that breeds idlers,” said China’s leader, Xi Jinping, in 2021. Many people still rely on their families.
