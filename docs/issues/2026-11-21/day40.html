Understanding the world through a wine glass A full-bodied history offers you Pliny the Elder as wine critic and other memorable anecdotes Nov 19th 2026 Have you thought about what you want on your tombstone? Darius I, who ruled the Achaemenid Persian empire—the largest the world had yet seen— reputedly highlighted a much smaller feat in his epitaph: “I was able to drink lots of wine, and handle it well.” In a globe-spanning new book, Kathleen Burk of University College London looks at how different cultures and people have handled, savoured and favoured their wine from the Stone Age to the present day. Despite wine’s modern associations with chateaux, haute cuisine and elites, for most of its history it has been an “egalitarian beverage”. That is because it used to be much safer to drink wine than water (alcohol can kill pathogens). It formed a big part of people’s diets—though it was doctored to

meet the tastes of the times. In ancient Greece and Rome wine was often diluted with water, to prevent rapid intoxication, and sometimes sweetened with condensed grape juice. Homer wrote approvingly in the “Iliad” of how a woman even mixed goat’s cheese and barley into red wine. (Who needs a cheese board on the side, if you can add one in?) Like an elixir-fuelled party, “Wine” leaves you with memorable anecdotes. Ms Burk argues that Pliny the Elder was the “Robert Parker of his day”, shaping views of good wine around 1,900 years before the founding of publications such as the Wine Advocate. Many people know about the French revolution’s connection to food (though Marie Antoinette never actually said “let them eat cake”), but there were protests over wine taxes that predated the storming of the Bastille, usually pinpointed as the revolution’s start. Though “Wine” is deeply researched and pleasurably wide-ranging, it can sometimes feel too academic. Still, the glass here is more than half-full: the book offers a compelling take on how central wine has been to culture and commerce. For a while, starting in the 11th century, it was probably the most widely traded commodity in Europe. Wine has flourished even in places you might not expect there to be a strong wine culture, such as the Middle East. Arabic has more than 100 terms for wine and separate verbs for drinking in the morning (and even for drinking as early as possible in the morning). Technology, including stronger glass and corks, broadened wine’s reach and lengthened its shelf life, by allowing bottles to be stored on their sides. (Previously wine bottles were shaped like onions.) Before corks were used as a bottle stopper in the 1500s, people used to pour olive oil in to block the air (the oil floated to the top). The custom of swirling your glass and tasting wine began because people wanted to make sure they had removed all the oil. Will wine be the drink of choice for people in the future? Ms Burk devotes some attention—though not enough—to the global decline in boozing. In France, for example, consumption of red wine has fallen by 90% since the 1970s. Winemakers are worried; should enthusiasts be, too? Maybe. But millions will take a passion for wine to the grave. ■

Why there are so many bizarre national days National Hermit Day, anyone? Or National Button Day? Nov 19th 2026 FOR AMERICANS, at least, November is the month of Thanksgiving. Each year, on the fourth Thursday, Americans are encouraged to feel grateful as they gather with family and gobble up 46m turkeys. Yet there are other, odder things that calendars suggest people should be thankful for in November. There is National Butter Day, Bison Day, Seatbelt Day and Button Day. (Forget zips and laces and celebrate “the function of buttons”!) If that all sounds a bit absurd, well, there is also National Absurdity Day on November 20th. There are more such national “holidays” than there are days in the year. They span the very serious (National Sanctity of Human Life Day) and the very silly (National Pretend to be a Time Traveller Day). There are scheduling conflicts, as when the day of Remembrance for Road Traffic

Victims coincides with Raisin Bran Cereal Day. Many have uninspiring names (National Carbonated Beverage with Caffeine Day), or lack inspiration altogether (National Eat a Cranberry Day falls just after National Cranberry Relish Day). Who is anointing all these days—and why? Proper national celebrations are approved by governments. The last time a federal holiday was made in America was in 2021, when Joe Biden formalised Juneteenth to commemorate the end of slavery. The United Nations designates global celebrations, such as International Women’s Day, to publicise causes. As you might expect, goofy days are easier to create. There are self-appointed day-makers, such as National Day Calendar, which acts like “Wikipedia for national days”, according to its founder, Marlo Anderson. It is free to submit a day to National Day Calendar, and anyone can do it. The company gets more than 30,000 applications a year, says Mr Anderson, and there is a “very tough” selection process so only a handful are chosen. (You can’t help but wonder, then, how National Barcode Day got through.) New days are often suggested by charities, or companies seeking to boost sales. Though they are called “national” days, they are promoted internationally: National Day Calendar helps brands advertise relevant days through their website, social channels and media such as television and newspapers. National days trend online. People sometimes post more about #PizzaDay or #AvocadoDay than about breaking news events. Yet such odd holidays predate the internet. In 1909 raisin-growers in California created National Raisin Day, sharing discounts and recipes to mark the occasion. When Father’s Day emerged in the early 20th century people complained it was a frivolous gimmick. Yet today other family-oriented days, such as National Siblings Day or National Daughter Day, are also popular. They certainly seem easier to celebrate than some of the more niche occasions. Dan Brotzel, a journalist, tried observing a silly holiday every day for a year. On National Hermit Day he interviewed a Catholic hermit (naturally, she had no idea the day existed); on National Cabbage Day he stared “very intently at cabbage for a while”. It
