How did “the most famous literary celebrity of an era” come to be forgotten? In a new book Jeanna Smialek, a journalist, endeavours to find out. Her interest in Edgeworth began after seeing her described in a book as perhaps “the first woman to think about economics”. She ordered a copy of “Castle Rackrent” and was surprised to find that the tale of an Anglo-Irish dynasty also contained a potted version of “The Wealth of Nations”. Even the title had an echo of Adam Smith’s seminal work: he wrote about “racked rent” as an extortionate charge that left tenant farmers with little money to improve their land. Economic concepts recur throughout Edgeworth’s fiction. In “Letters for Literary Ladies” she likened the exclusion of women from the educated class to the creation of artificial scarcity in markets. “The Cherry Orchard” touched on the division of labour. “Ennui” examined how incentives shape behaviour. “Because she cloaked her ideas in fiction”, Edgeworth “managed to engage with the most intense debates of the era without transgressing the strict social codes of the time”. From a young age, Edgeworth was reading Smith and Jean-Jacques Rousseau. By 14, her father had her helping run their estate in Ireland. The Edgeworths implemented all kinds of reforms: they paid high wages, eliminated unpaid “duty work”, restructured leases and promoted education. The estate gave her a chance to see how economic theories worked in practice. Edgeworth met Thomas Malthus and David Ricardo in London and struck up a friendship with the two economists; in their correspondence they sparred about the limits of free trade and other things. Ms Smialek ably recreates the world of these 19th-century thinkers and unearths interesting details (such as Ricardo’s tendency to do an embarrassing jig at parties). It is with a more queasy sense of foreboding that you read Ricardo arguing in 1822—about 20 years before the famine—that it was imprudent for Ireland to rely on a single, cheap crop that was prone to failure. Given that Edgeworth helped economics “to gain widespread popularity” through her novels and assisted in “advancing and altering its earliest theories”, why is her work overlooked? As the discipline became more technical in the late 19th century, contributions made through literature and

philosophy came to be seen as less valuable. “Mainstream economics had refashioned itself as a science,” Ms Smialek writes. “Its practitioners would treat it like physics, creating rules and principles that they argued would apply always and everywhere.” At the same time, Edgeworth’s didactic literary style fell out of fashion. She was interested in characters mostly for the ideas they could convey rather than the feelings they could stir up. (She didn’t think much of “Emma” when Austen sent her favourite author an early copy.) “Just as economics had become the domain of maths and reason, literature had become the domain of sentiment and empathy,” Ms Smialek argues. “There was no room for Maria, who mixed the two, in either.” But “The Invisible Hand of Maria Edgeworth” will make readers appreciate her fingerprints. ■ This article was downloaded by zlibrary from https://www.economist.com/culture/2026/11/19/the-most-important-economics-mind- youve-never-heard-of

The oddball who founded UPS—and changed the world James Casey’s obsession with efficiency helped make next-day delivery possible Nov 19th 2026 JAMES CASEY is far from an easy subject for a biographer. Unlike many modern business titans, the founder of the United Parcel Service (UPS) was reclusive. He wrote no memoirs and left behind no letters when he died, aged 95, in 1983. He seems to have had no interests other than his work: he spent his Sundays driving delivery routes in his Packard, comparing his times with those expected of drivers. The New Yorker, in a rare profile, said that “he talks only about parcels.” Anyone writing about Casey, then, must do two things: doggedly hunt for information and make a dull person’s life interesting. Marc Levinson, a historian, economist and former editor at this newspaper, succeeds on both

counts. Just as his previous book, “The Box”, used the shipping container to tell the story of globalisation, this one explores how Casey changed the world of commerce. Today, people in the rich world expect that anything they buy online will show up on their doorsteps the next day. “More than 300,000 packages are delivered somewhere in the world every minute,” Mr Levinson observes. Casey built a fledgling messenger service into a Fortune 500 firm, and changed how people shop. (If you have ever successfully ordered a Christmas gift at the last minute, you have him to thank.) In 1907 Casey’s first business was one of many in Seattle, his hometown, that delivered packages from department stores to buyers’ houses. With messengers taking each item in turn from shop to customer, and each short trip fetching a few cents, it was difficult to turn a profit. So Casey decided to consolidate. He had retailers send all their outgoing items to a central location, where they would be sorted by destination. This made delivery routes more efficient; it also allowed him to charge a premium for a same- day delivery service. He offered retailers long-term contracts, which saved them from having to spend money on operations (which, in the early 20th century, meant horses and wagons). For Casey, it guaranteed volume and financial surety; and made it harder for rivals to poach his customers. He was relentless in seeking better ways to work. In the 1920s Casey hired an engineer who had worked at General Motors and Ford to design machinery that would help sort parcels. Trucks were loaded in reverse order of delivery, so that drivers wasted no time scrabbling around for the right item. UPS sent observers armed with stopwatches on deliveries in order to calculate an “ideal performance” for each trip. That benchmark was then used to award bonuses to drivers who made more deliveries than expected. Similar methods are now commonplace at companies such as Amazon. But it was Casey who first embraced automation and an “unapologetic insistence on efficient procedures”. And competitors “were to be harassed or simply eradicated”. UPS hounded challengers by filing umpteen “complaints, protests and objections”. It

flouted antitrust laws by secretly buying up rivals, locking retailers into exclusive contracts and striking deals to carve up markets with competitors. It also persuaded the authorities that “regulations should…be tailored to its needs alone,” winning exemptions from rules that constrained other firms. In that regard, Casey was again ahead of his time. He was a “demanding taskmaster” with a ruthless, “win-at-all-costs” mentality—the kind that has become de rigueur in Silicon Valley and beyond. The lesson from this book, budding entrepreneurs may feel, is that you should stop at nothing to deliver the goods. ■ This article was downloaded by zlibrary from https://www.economist.com/culture/2026/11/19/the-oddball-who-founded-ups-and- changed-the-world
