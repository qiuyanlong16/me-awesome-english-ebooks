Jensen Huang’s fall is bad news for Silicon Valley’s accelerationists Already their movement was on the back foot Nov 19th 2026 For the “accelerationists” in Silicon Valley—who are eager to ensure artificial intelligence continues developing at breakneck speed—the fall of Jensen Huang is a heavy blow. Nvidia’s boss was the movement’s most credible spokesperson. In one of his final interviews before his resignation, Mr Huang decried the “alarmist” voices stoking anxiety over the technology’s pace of improvement, and argued against regulating AI. Fellow accelerationists—among them Elon Musk, boss of SpaceX; Martin Casado, a partner at Andreessen Horowitz; and Garry Tan, head of Y Combinator—have been among the loudest voices calling for Mr Huang’s return. Already the group had taken a beating in the midterm elections, which will soon elevate dozens of AI-sceptics to power. The landmark

listing of Anthropic, an AI lab that calls at every opportunity for regulation, lest its work end humanity, is yet another setback. All this has given jitters to the open-source AI community, which has benefited from the support of the accelerationists. Colette Kress, Nvidia’s monotone interim boss, is unlikely to imitate her predecessor’s fiery opposition to AI “doomers”. Mr Huang publicly bemoaned their talk of “science-fiction outcomes” and hinted at unspecified “ulterior reasons” for spreading fear; of Dario Amodei, Anthropic’s boss, he said they disagreed on “almost everything”. Mr Huang’s opinions found sympathetic ears in the White House. He grew close with Donald Trump over the past two years, with the president stating on November 16th that he “would not mind” Mr Huang being reinstated. That looks unlikely, creating nervousness in the open-source AI community. It was already on edge over growing calls for American firms to be banned from using free Chinese models. In July Mr Huang came out against that effort, with Nvidia and more than 230 other organisations signing a letter urging the government to avoid “premature restrictions”. Whether Nvidia “will continue to aggressively promote the development of this ecosystem” in Mr Huang’s absence is an “open question”, says Anastasios Angelopoulos, boss of Arena, a platform for ranking AI models. Mr Huang’s support for open-source AI went beyond policy statements. He was also one of its biggest funders, using Nvidia’s cash to invest in and acquire companies in the field. In September Nvidia said it would buy Hugging Face, a library of open-source models, for $12.9bn. It has also invested in Reflection and Thinking Machine Labs, two makers of open- source models, and developed a line of its own, called Nemotron. Commercial logic may lead Nvidia to continue its support for the approach. On this week’s earnings call, Ms Kress said that the company remained “committed to fostering a vibrant open-model ecosystem”. Whether it will continue investing at the same pace is less clear. Ms Kress noted that the company was “rigorously evaluating” future investments. Still, others in Silicon Valley would welcome a steadier pace. Daniel Yanisse, boss of Checkr, which offers AI-powered background checks, says

he and many of his peers are ready for some deceleration, arguing that it would give more time for the technology to demonstrate real value without the pressure created by the accelerationists, who promise an imminent utopian future. The global south’s smartphone king goes big on AI Faced with stiffening competition and rising costs, Transsion plans its next act Nov 19th 2026 On the streets of Nairobi, smartphones branded Tecno, Infinix or itel are a common sight. The sleek devices are all made by Transsion, a Chinese company that is the biggest phone-seller in the global south by volume. Last year it shifted 170m handsets, roughly two-thirds as many as Apple. In Africa more than half of phones purchased are Transsion devices. The company has thrived by making phones at scale, tailored in price and design to its chosen markets. But with competition intensifying and the artificial-intelligence boom pushing up the cost of semiconductors, it must do more. So it is venturing into new products—and making its own bet on AI.

Transsion’s success has rested on three pillars. First, unlike many of its Chinese rivals, it went global early. Transsion’s founder first started doing business in Africa in the 2000s, and when he set up his current company in 2013 the continent was its focus. A few years later it expanded into India and South-East Asia. Today it sells handsets in over 100 countries through a vast web of 2,900 distributors, helped by ties with local telecoms providers. Second is the tailoring. Transsion’s handsets typically cost less than a tenth as much as an iPhone, making them attractive to customers with little money to spend on a device. They are also designed to suit local needs. For Africa, where power outages are frequent, Transsion has focused on long battery life and speedy charging. It has also developed “sweat-resistant” USB ports that better cope with the hot climate. For gaming-mad South-East Asia, it has developed cooling cases, sold as a separate accessory. (The company’s built- in gaming app has 140m monthly users.) Third is scale. Transsion makes about 40% of its smartphones in six factories of its own (four in China, one each in Bangladesh and Ethiopia) and outsources the rest to 17 contractors, mostly in China. Thanks in part to this hybrid set-up, the phone-maker has proved nimble, taking four to eight months to develop its smartphones.
