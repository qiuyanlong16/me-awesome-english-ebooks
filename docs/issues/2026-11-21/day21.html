Russia and Ukraine are bombing bridges. Who’s winning? A setback for Russia in Crimea, but damage in Kyiv may matter more Nov 19th 2026 THIS WAS the week when the war in Ukraine became a battle of bridges. Four crossings in all, 725km apart. Two systems of fighting: one creative and carefully orchestrated, the other blunt but just as effective. The headlines focused on Ukraine’s partial destruction of the bridge at Kerch, a $3.6bn monument to Russia’s annexation of Crimea. That wounded both Vladimir Putin’s vanity and his logistics. But Russia’s ballistic strikes on Kyiv’s river bridges, which damaged two vital crossings, could count for more. The scale of destruction at Kerch emerged in early dawn. Images released by the Ukrainians showed the road bridge’s suspension arch and navigation span lying in the water, blocking Russia’s shipping channel to the Azov sea. The rail bridge was damaged enough to limit its use to a single track. It was

not the only good news for Ukraine. Farther north, its forces severed a crossing on the road linking Russia to Crimea, a vital artery for resupply to Russia’s southern military grouping. The strikes were like two claws closing on the peninsula. Volodymyr Zelensky, Ukraine’s president, called them the finale of a “seven-stage operation”. Those involved say they show how good the Ukrainians have become at combined cruise-missile and drone operations. Their forces meticulously peeled away air defences in Crimea, including the security system around the bridge, before launching waves of night attacks. “Our pilots have become so good, so exact, that you don’t even see a leaf fall from a nearby tree when they impact,” says one Ukrainian commander. In Kyiv the method was cruder. Seven Russian ballistic missiles cut through starved air defences to partially destroy two bridges joining the western and eastern banks of the city. Road and metro links were severed, and some suspension cables were torn loose. The morning commute turned grim for hundreds of thousands, with traffic jams stretching for kilometres and pedestrians stumbling across what remained of the bridges in the rain. Russia had struck before, with both drones and missiles. But these attacks were the most damaging so far.

Ukrainian air defences face a losing battle this winter, with interceptors running low and what appears to be a deliberate American policy to hold back deliveries. But there is one area where they seem to be winning. In the summer a new generation of Russian remote-controlled drones threatened to bring the capital to a standstill by targeting infrastructure with pinpoint accuracy. Today, they have been blunted by a system of f16 fighters, drone interceptors and what has been called a protective “dome” over Kyiv. The dome, a network of jammers designed to confuse signals from drones and the communications networks they work on, was installed over the capital in September. It is part of a broader system, making its contribution hard to assess. But December One, a firm that made most of the jammers, says the results speak for themselves. “Like everyone else in Kyiv I was thinking about moving my family in September,” says Illia Kabachynsky, a company official. “Now, pinpoint jet-drone hits have practically disappeared.” The success of Kyiv’s anti-drone system has pushed Russia to double down on its ballistic missiles. It has hundreds of these in reserve, and a steady production rate. That has led some to question the wisdom of the decision to attack one of Mr Putin’s most sensitive projects, given the risk of him lashing out in response. Captain Dmitry Pletenchuk, spokesman for the navy, which helped attack the Kerch bridge, dismisses any idea of tit-for-tat provocation. “We cannot keep looking over our shoulder to think what the enemy might think of us,” he says. Russia was already attacking civilian ships, blocking Ukraine’s vital shipping lanes. Much of its campaign was co-ordinated from Crimea. Hitting the Kerch bridge just before the winter also makes quick repairs impossible. “It will be spring before they manage a fix,” he predicts. Andrej Babis tries to curtail public broadcasting Hundreds of thousands protested in the biggest demonstration since the fall of communism Nov 19th 2026 It was the biggest demonstration in Prague since the Velvet Revolution, the non-violent protests that toppled communism in 1989. On a sunny November 15th, as many as 400,000 Czechs gathered on Letna plain next to Prague Castle to protest against proposed changes to the funding of public- service broadcasters. They waved Czech, European and Ukrainian flags and held signs saying “We refuse the Slovak scenario”. Many wore triangular paper hats similar to Vecernícek, a character from a children’s show on public television, who has become a symbol of the fight for independent public broadcasting. The demonstration was the latest and biggest in a series held in 21 Czech towns, following a strike of public-service television and radio staff on

November 3rd. The protests began when the government of Andrej Babis, the prime minister, rushed through a draft bill in an overnight parliamentary session in late October to scrap mandatory licence fees (which amount to 205 koruna or $9 a month for radio and TV) and replace them with funding from the public budget. But they have turned into something bigger, an expression of deep discontent with Mr Babis, the Freedom and Direct Democracy party (SPD) and the Motorists, his populist-right coalition partners. “For the first time since the Velvet Revolution Czechs are really worried about their democracy,” says Erik Tabery, the editor-in-chief of Respekt, a weekly. Mr Babis had already chipped away at the independence of public-service broadcasters during his first term in office between 2017 and 2021. He accused in particular Czech Television (CT) of bias against him. In 2020 lawmakers from ANO (“Yes”), his party, elected several outspoken critics of CT to the Czech Television Council, which is responsible for appointing the broadcaster’s director-general and overseeing its budget. “This is a continuation of what he did then”, says Vaclav Stetka of the European New School of Digital Studies in Frankfurt an der Oder. Although Mr Babis has not fulfilled campaign promises of cheaper energy and a lower pension age, ANO is still the biggest party, polling around 30%. Yet fringe parties in his coalition, whose leaders are seen as arrogant and incompetent, are viscerally disliked, says Mr Stetka. At recent elections for the Senate the ruling coalition failed to win a single seat, a result Mr Babis called “catastrophic”. Mr Babis is likely to retain his draft bill but may agree to changes. The issue is whether that will be enough to satisfy protesters who worry that the Czech Republic may be following Slovakia,where in 2023, just before Robert Fico, the authoritarian prime minister, returned to power, the licence fee was replaced with stingier state-budget funding. In 2024 Radio and Television of Slovakia (RTVS), was renamed Slovak Television and Radio (STVR), with a board appointed by the government. Popular programmes were cut and critical journalists fired. Today Slovak public broadcasting is a shadow of its former self. In the 2026 World Press Freedom Index by Reporters without Borders, Slovakia ranks 37th. The Czech Republic still ranks 11th.

The pro-Russian Mr Fico faces elections in September next year. He is looking more vulnerable after losing his majority in parliament following the defection of an independent lawmaker in early November. Mr Babis, however, is determined to serve out his full term until 2029. One reason is that he enjoys being shielded by parliamentary immunity from standing trial in a long-running case involving alleged abuse of European Union subsidies. Morover, he is not term-limited. The 72-year-old wants to stick around and could yet choose to run again in 2029 or later, he has said on Sorry jako, his video podcast. Two days after the big protest on Letna plain, Czechs celebrated the anniversary of the Velvet Revolution. Mr Babis skipped the festivities in favour of a working trip to Israel. He risked being booed had he stayed at home. In December protesters will take to the streets again.
