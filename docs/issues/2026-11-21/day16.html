They point to surging data use at home, as well as a desire among both Chinese and American tech giants to plonk more data centres outside their domestic jurisdictions. To take full advantage, countries in the region will have to persuade tech firms that they are reliable partners, and ensure local populations stay supportive. They will also have to navigate the heated rivalry between the world’s technological superpowers. Growing local demand is the primary driver. Most of Latin America’s data is still stored and processed in the United States. That was tolerable when few people were online. Yet internet adoption in the region is now near 80%, higher than in China. Brazilians spend more than nine hours per day on the internet, compared with fewer than seven hours for Americans, according to a report by Atlántico, a consultancy. This causes lags. Content from North Virginia, a global hub, takes 150 milliseconds to reach São Paulo. Content from Vinhedo takes two. Many companies see value in paying to narrow the gap, says Raimundo Diaz of Vistra, an accounting firm. Yet that is not the only play. Some big tech companies view locations in Latin America as important nodes in networks they are creating to serve users all around the world. ByteDance, the Chinese owner of TikTok, is investing up to $39bn in a huge data-centre campus in north-east Brazil, apparently enticed in part by undersea cables that run from there to Europe, Africa and points elsewhere. Some companies are scouting for sites that are suitable for training AI models (for which proximity to the ultimate end-user is not crucial). In that kind of business Latin America will find itself competing for capital “against Canada, the Nordic countries, the Middle East and South-East Asia”, says David Dias of EY, a consultancy.

Latin American countries are busily talking up their advantages—over each other, and over hubs farther afield. Many can offer cut-price energy, which is also green (see chart). Lots of hydropower means 85% of Brazil’s electricity is renewable. In Chile, which has acres of solar panels, 68% is. Having plenty of affordable electricity can also reduce water consumption in data centres (by permitting the use of water-efficient cooling systems, which come with the downside of being power-hungry). Construction is generally affordable, too. Building data centres in Silicon Valley costs $14 per watt, according to a study by Turner & Townsend, a consultancy. Latin American rates range from $11.5 per watt in Mexico to $9 in Bogotá, the capital of Colombia. Brazil has around 700MW of data-centre capacity, close to half the total in Latin America. By 2030 that could rise to as much as 5GW, predicts Alessandro Lombardi, the head of Elea Data Centres, one of the companies building them. The country’s president, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, is keen; before the presidential election in October he ushered through a reform slashing an exorbitant import duty that Brazil has been imposing on IT equipment. “Bring the data centres here,” demanded Lula, as the president is known, during his victory speech.

Factories in Mexico have recently made a killing assembling servers destined for data centres in the United States (where, because of local opposition, it is becoming much harder to build new ones). The country would now like to add to its own 300MW of data-centre capacity, much of it clustered in the arid central state of Querétaro, north of the capital. Chile can offer investor-friendly rules modelled on Singapore’s, says Natalia López of Chile Data Centres, a trade group; Colombia is touting the world’s first free-trade zone for hyperscalers. Paraguay does moonshots, such as a $12bn plan for an American plant near the thunderous Iguazú Falls. Even Argentina, a fiscal basket-case, has seen interest in the southern province of Patagonia, which is sparsely populated and contains Vaca Muerta, a huge patch of shale gas. Yet to succeed Latin American countries will have to clear away a heap of obstacles. Some of these are technical. The sites with the best internet connectivity are often not the same as those in which electricity is most abundant. Even when energy is plentiful, spotty transmission can lead to blackouts, says Tomás Ocampo of Unblock energy, an Argentine startup that uses gas that would otherwise be flared to power data centres. Permitting can be cumbersome, with long waiting times to connect to the grid. Technology firms worry that, although the region’s governments have been lining up to promise tax breaks, they can often sound flaky on crucial matters such as intellectual-property rights. Though Latin Americans are much less averse to data centres than peers in the United States, public opinion could shift as investment ramps up. Sporadic protests against new projects in the region have already begun to take place. “There’s a growing sense of worry,” says Fabro Steibel of ITS-Rio, a Brazilian think-tank. In the background looms geopolitics. That came to the fore suddenly on November 16th when the US Commerce Department declared that Brazilian data centres would need a licence from them to import the most advanced semiconductors. This reflects a worry that they will become a way for China to bypass America’s export controls, by letting Chinese companies remotely train AI models using the latest kit. President Donald Trump has long demanded that Latin America curb Chinese investment in critical infrastructure, notes Bruna Santos of the Inter-American Dialogue, a

Washington-based think-tank. Mr Trump could well begin insisting they turn away Chinese money for data centres too. The whole world wants Colombians to have their babies Lawmakers are struggling to regulate a boom in international surrogacy Nov 19th 2026 IN 2019 MARK and his husband were ready to become fathers. But commercial surrogacy is not available in Ireland, where they lived. Going to America was too expensive; cheaper options such as Ukraine and Georgia excluded gay couples. Another couple suggested a less conventional destination: their son had been born in Colombia. So in the end they travelled to Bogotá, the capital, where an agency arranged an egg donor, IVF treatment, a surrogate and legal services. A growing number of foreigners are turning to Colombia to help them start families. Mark Semple, who runs ReproVida, a medical group, recalls that the industry was “tiny” when he set up there in 2018. It exploded in the following years as gay couples in particular began arriving “largely through

word of mouth”. A second wave followed Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, which disrupted what had been sizeable surrogacy industries in both of those countries. That cataclysm had the effect of pushing lots of heterosexual couples towards Colombia, too. Perhaps as many as 2,100 arrangements took place in Colombia in 2025, up from 1,600 in 2021. That makes it one of the world’s main destinations for international commercial surrogacy, reckons William Houghton of Sensible Surrogacy, a consultancy. The country’s advantages include relatively sophisticated fertility clinics, particularly in the capital, combined with low costs. A typical surrogacy programme costs around $65,000 in Colombia, compared with $110,000 in Mexico (Latin America’s other big surrogacy hub) and roughly $200,000 in the United States, estimates Mr Houghton. That the country has an ethnically diverse population is another draw: it means that in cases in which egg donation is required, parents can more easily find a local donor who shares their own physical characteristics. In much of Latin America legal restrictions have prevented international surrogacy industries from taking root. Colombia is different: the industry there was engendered by a 2009 ruling of the Constitutional Court, which established that there were no laws prohibiting the practice. At the time, the justices recommended that surrogates should not provide their own eggs, and that arrangements should not be motivated by profit—though those strictures were never actually written into the statute books. The lack of regulation is both a help and a hindrance to the industry—and to those who are relying on it to create their families. Judges struggling to apply existing family laws have sometimes reached conflicting conclusions in disputes over surrogacy, says Santiago Martínez, a lawyer at Martínez Díaz & Asociados, which advises on such cases. In one recent incident a surrogate in the city of Medellín declined to hand a baby over to its biological father. She initially won custody, only for an appeals court to overturn that decision this year. Many clinics and agencies select surrogates carefully. One Colombian lawyer estimates that only one in ten applicants are accepted at the clinics he works with. But growing demand is making suitable candidates more difficult to find. A few years ago it would take about four months for

agencies to match parents with a suitable surrogate; now some end up waiting as long as a year. The shortage is increasing the incentive for unscrupulous operators to cut corners, for example by accepting women who fail medical or psychological screening, or not screening them at all. Weak oversight has left women vulnerable to exploitation, particularly those from poorer areas such as Cúcuta, near the Venezuelan border. Alejandra Vera of Corporación Mujer Denuncia y Muévete, a women’s-rights organisation, says some clinics recruit women in desperate financial situations through social media. Some Venezuelan migrants in Colombia say they were asked to sign surrogacy contracts in English that they could not understand. Some contracts have reportedly restricted women’s movements, diets or sexual relationships, or sought to deny them the right to an abortion, which is protected under Colombia’s constitution. Such stories have intensified calls for tighter restrictions. In July Congress passed a law adding “reproductive exploitation” to the forms of exploitation covered by human-trafficking legislation (without clearly defining the term). A draft bill introduced in August seeks to ban surrogacy altogether. Opposition comes from both feminists on the left, who argue that surrogacy commodifies women’s bodies and exploits poorer women, and religious conservatives on the right, who believe it undermines the traditional family. A ban looks improbable, all the same. Congress has long struggled to settle divisive moral questions, says Mr Martínez. Over a dozen bills attempting to regulate surrogacy in Colombia have failed over the past two decades. So the most likely scenario is that the business continues muddling along as at present. As a result, parents will continue to be open to the risk of lengthy legal battles, while too many surrogates will be left without firm protections. Colombia’s courts helped make the country a pioneer in reproductive freedom.
