The next stop for Europe’s failed asylum-seekers: Rwanda The plan is the latest sign of hardening migration policy across the continent Nov 19th 2026 “ATRULY HISTORIC paradigm shift”. “A game-changer”. “One big milestone”. If officials are cautious about overselling Europe’s asylum deal with Rwanda, no one told the politicians from five European governments who unveiled it in Vienna on November 16th. “This is the first real answer anyone has put on the table to solve the deep problems with the current system,” said Mette Frederiksen, Denmark’s prime minister. Under the plan, which is promised to take effect next year, migrants in the five countries—Austria, Denmark, Germany, Greece and the Netherlands— whose asylum bids have been rejected may be transferred to a “return hub” in Rwanda. They will be able to integrate into local communities, return

home or move to another country ready to accept them, said Vincent Biruta, Rwanda’s interior minister, speaking with European ministers whom his government had assiduously courted. Officials say Rwanda will now sign bilateral agreements with all five governments. The Rwanda plan is a new element of Europe’s migration “turnaround”. Thanks partly to calmer conditions near its borders, irregular arrivals have plummeted (see chart 1). Last month saw the fewest asylum claims in Germany since October 2011. An “asylum pact” that took effect in June helps. But it is hard to deport asylum-seekers whose bids have failed. Lacking documentation, or coming from countries that do not co-operate, many fall into the shadows. The EU issues far more return orders than it carries out (see chart 2 ). Politicians want to show they can do things previously seen as impossible, says Raphael Bossong from the German Institute for International and Security Affairs in Berlin. But where populist-right ones demand “remigration”, the mainstream seeks a more orderly approach. In October the EU passed a law allowing the creation of return hubs, with human-rights and monitoring safeguards. That, say officials, puts the scheme on a sounder legal footing than other efforts. Britain’s ill-fated 2022 deal, which unlike

the European scheme would have sent migrants to Rwanda to process their asylum claims, cost taxpayers £715m ($910m) before being abandoned. Details are scant and questions remain. The first is effectiveness. The few hundred migrants at most who could be sent to Rwanda next year will not make a dent in the stock of failed asylum-seekers in Europe. Germany alone has over 250,000 migrants served with return orders. The “bigger number”, Ms Frederiksen said, would be migrants who, hearing of the Rwanda scheme, might decide not to travel to Europe. But would minds really be changed by the remote possibility of being sent to Rwanda? “Evidence of a deterrent effect is highly uncertain,” said a British official of his country’s scheme, which had a more credible claim to deterrence since it would have applied to more migrants. Second, what would actually happen to those sent to Rwanda, one of Africa’s poorer and most densely populated countries? Human-rights groups fear, once migrants are beyond Europe’s gaze, they could face mistreatment or further deportation, including refoulement into danger. “This is a hugely repressive regime,” says Tineke Strik, a Dutch Green member of the European Parliament. But such concerns rarely trouble European officials attacked by critics they see as naive do-gooders. “Unless you put a hub in Liechtenstein you’ll run out of options,” sniffs one. The new deal has a role

for United Nations migration and refugee agencies in offering advice, monitoring conditions and aiding integration. Rwanda already hosts EU- funded centres for refugees who have been evacuated from Libya. Even so, legal challenges to the new plan are certain. Others worry what the deal may mean for relations with Rwanda, which has supported a murderous militia in the Democratic Republic of Congo and persecutes dissidents (one was recently found beheaded in Uganda). “It is deeply troubling that European governments are considering sending rejected asylum-seekers to Rwanda,” says Rémy Amahirwa, a Sweden- based Rwandan whose mother, an opposition figure, has been imprisoned by Paul Kagame’s government. Last year the German government cancelled talks with Rwanda because of its actions in Congo. Europeans want to distinguish Rwanda’s treatment of migrants from its broader human-rights record. Yet some envoys say they already detect signs of diplomatic reticence from European governments. Beyond diplomatic cover, what is in it for Mr Kagame? Mr Biruta said Rwanda was just doing its bit to manage the problem of irregular immigration. In reality it hopes for cash, aid projects and a reputation as a reliable partner. It also wants to select who comes, with a preference for professionals who can integrate into labour markets and boost the local economy. “They feel bruised from the UK arrangements, so there is a real interest to make this work,” says one European official. Not everyone in Europe is on board. In Germany doubts among Social Democrats, the junior ruling coalition partner, extend into the cabinet. France and Spain, among others, remain sceptical. Yet both countries may elect right-wing governments during the next six months. Europe’s rightward march on migration policy is likely to continue. Other EU countries, notably Italy, are clamouring to join the return-hub plan —even Britain may be interested—and European governments are reportedly negotiating with other potential return-hub hosts. Meanwhile, plans are being drawn up to avoid a repeat of the scenes earlier this year, when tens of thousands of Moroccans swarmed into the Spanish exclave of Ceuta. Frontex, Europe’s border-guard agency, may have its mandate expanded; it may also help with deportations to Rwanda. Ultimately some

governments aspire to externalise the entire asylum process.
