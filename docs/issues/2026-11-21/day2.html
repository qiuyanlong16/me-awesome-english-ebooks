Deeper collusion between hostile powers threatens the West China, Russia, Iran and North Korea are bent on making a world safe for autocrats Nov 19th 2026 In February 1945 Winston Churchill, Franklin Roosevelt and Joseph Stalin met in Yalta to decide the planet’s future. As their armies pressed for victory against Nazi Germany, the leaders of Britain, America and the Soviet Union carved up Europe into what became communist and capitalist blocs. Historians differ as to whether the Allies could have struck a better deal, but no one doubts that the meeting was historic. Unlike this week’s photo opportunity in Shenzhen for Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin. Described by some as the new Yalta, it is nothing of the sort. President Trump, fresh from humiliation in the midterms, wants to look strong again on television. Russia’s despot, humiliated by years of failure to conquer his

smaller neighbour, wants to rub shoulders with real superpowers. China’s leader is happy to appear as the gracious emperor welcoming squabbling foreigners to his villa. But little of substance is likely to emerge. Mr Trump's technique of winging it will not sway the tightly scripted Mr Xi. The real story in geopolitics is the deepening collaboration between four autocratic, anti-Western regimes: China, Russia, Iran and North Korea. This is not new: Mr Trump’s first defence secretary identified the threat in 2018. It is not a formal alliance, either, and its members are very different. China is an economic and technological colossus, as the dancing robots and ubiquitous delivery drones of Shenzhen make clear. Russia’s is a middling economy; Iran and North Korea are midgets. Iran is a theocracy; China and North Korea are godless, unless you count Kim Jong Un. In Beijing, Moscow and Pyongyang, one man rules; in Tehran it is unclear who does. But all four regimes share a core interest: they want a world safe for autocrats, specifically themselves. And as Mr Trump weakens America’s wider and deeper network of alliances, America’s foes are quietly helping each other. The most obvious collusion is military. When Russia ravages Ukrainian cities, it does so with Iranian drone technology and Chinese components. Iranian engineers study how Russia fine-tunes their murderous designs. North Korea has sent some 16,000 soldiers to fight and die for Mr Putin, in return for cash and military technology. China stops short of direct military aid, but is soaking up data on how Russian drones perform in combat— handy for a superpower that has not fought a serious war since 1979. Meanwhile, the missiles Iran aims at American forces in the Gulf can be fired quickly (thanks to Chinese precursor chemicals for solid fuel) and accurately (thanks to Russian intelligence and Chinese satellite data). Economic collaboration is deepening, too. Trade with China keeps the other three economies afloat. Chinese payments systems offer ways to dodge Western sanctions. More than 95% of Russia’s trade with China is now in yuan or roubles, up from around 25% five years ago. Russian oil flows to China and North Korea; Iranian oil is shipped to China, largely via a “shadow fleet” of sanctions-busting ships that has grown by 1,500% since 2022. North Korean workers toil in Russian factories, easing the labour

shortage caused by Mr Putin sending so many Russians to their deaths in Ukraine. Axis diplomats scratch one another’s backs. They vote in harmony at the UN, where Russian and Chinese vetoes block any serious moves to restrain Mr Putin’s wars. Axis propagandists amplify one another’s narratives on screens everywhere, and promote stories that undermine Western democracies. Those who doubt the importance of the axis of malice note that the interests of its constituent regimes often fail to align. Iran, North Korea and Russia are rogue states; China is far more cautious. Having prospered hugely from globalisation, it does not want the American-led rules-based order to be replaced by chaos. It has long frowned on North Korea’s destabilisation of its backyard. And unlike oil-producing Russia, it suffers when Iran’s war with America drives up oil prices. Yet although they do not always pull in the same direction, these four regimes increasingly find ways to benefit from their shared antagonism towards America. China can cope with the irritation of costly oil, and sees great advantage if American aircraft-carriers are stuck in the Gulf instead of patrolling the Pacific. It does not want Mr Putin to start a wider war in Europe—Europeans are valuable customers for Chinese goods—but it is happy to see the West bogged down defending Ukraine with resources that might otherwise go to support Taiwan. And as Mr Putin deepens his ties with Kim Jong Un, China is less inclined to scold North Korea for its nuclear troublemaking; Mr Kim is adept at playing one friend off against another. There are precedents for informal collusion between geographically dispersed and culturally disparate authoritarian regimes. The most alarming is the original axis in the 1930s. Fascist Germany, Italy and Japan did not love one another, but they all felt aggrieved by the dominant powers. Germany and Japan did not fight arm in arm, but by expanding their empires at the same time, they offered the West two huge challenges at once, thus improving their own chances of victory. Today, if China decides to invade Taiwan, there is a good chance that Russia, Iran or North Korea will provoke a simultaneous crisis elsewhere, or in cyberspace.

This analogy goes only so far. Mr Putin, despite the hundreds of thousands of deaths he has caused, is not Hitler. But it would be rash for today’s democracies to ignore the threat posed by the axis of malice. Their systems of government seem unlikely to change soon. (Their citizens do not get a say.) With each passing day, their hackers have access to more powerful AI tools, and it is uncertain whether the West’s defences will keep up. Authoritarian systems are not as good as liberal democracies at generating wealth, fostering innovation or allowing their people decent lives. But when they collude, they can make the world darker and more dangerous.
