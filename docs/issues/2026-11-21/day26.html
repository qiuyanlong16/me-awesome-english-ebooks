Britain

America has long been Britain’s greatest cultural export. From Victorian authors to 1960s musicians, British creatives have had a knack for repackaging American culture and making Americans wild for it. Forget about politicians, military types and spooks: this is the real special relationship. Better than selling ice to Inuits, Britain sells America to Americans. Rockstar is just the latest to do so. Others include actual rockstars. Appropriating American rock, rhythm and blues made the Beatles and the Rolling Stones superstars. “Top Gun”, a 1980s film easily mistaken for American propaganda, was directed by Tony Scott—an advertising guru from Tyneside. “American Idol” and “America’s Got Talent”, reality-TV shows that embody the American showbiz dream, were dreamed up by British producers. Even all-American superheroes are often played by British actors. Think of Batman (Robert Pattinson), Spider-Man (Tom Holland) and Superman (Henry Cavill). Whereas “Top Gun” celebrates American machismo, “GTA” is a satire: a franchise built on an extremist’s interpretation of liberal individualism. Life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness? Why bother, when you can have money, excessive consumption and an inalienable right to sling grenades at pedestrians? Mouths water over 40,000-calorie “heartstopper” burgers. Pensioners fornicate on “senile isle”. “I’m living the dream, baby,” says the protagonist of “GTA V”, “and that dream is fucked.” Americans seem not to mind. “GTA VI”, like “GTA V” and “GTA IV” before it, may well be the biggest entertainment release to date. Analysts reckon it earned back the roughly $1.5bn it cost from pre-orders alone. Within weeks it will probably eclipse the combined $3.7bn, in today’s money, garnered at box offices by Christopher Nolan’s “Batman” trilogy (yet more American stories told by a Briton). Most sales will be to Americans, enamoured of the caricature Britain has painted of them. Britain is at once close enough to understand America and sufficiently distant to tease it, like a cheeky older relative with a faintly superior manner. Take a speech by King Charles III at the White House. Most world leaders try to flatter Donald Trump. Britain’s head of state likened the president’s new ballroom to Britain’s attempt to redevelop (read: burn down) the White

House in 1814. Shortly after that Fanny Trollope, an English writer, toured the American frontier. Her 1832 travelogue, “Domestic Manners of the Americans”, passed harsh judgment: “I would infinitely prefer sharing the apartment of a party of well-conditioned pigs.” Americans were cross. They still bought her book. Like other aspects of the special relationship, these cultural critiques mostly run one way. For American strategists, Britain is a useful airstrip—a tea break en route to the Gulf. For American creatives, targeting the British market is akin to reaching for pennies rather than pounds. Besides, most Americans simply do not think about Britain. Those who do imagine a land of live-action period drama, or a corner of Christendom fallen to sharia. Britons, however, are America-mad. Americans dominate both the British charts and the box office. YouGov, a pollster, puts four Americans in its list of the ten celebrities Britons are most likely to recognise. There are only that number of Britons in America’s top 100. As outsiders who have long fixated on America—a condition with which The Economist sympathises—Britons are uniquely placed to celebrate its triumphs and satirise its excesses. For Britain, the special relationship serves as an invaluable source of escapism. Why face the banality of your own decline when you can laugh at America’s absurdity instead? “GTA London”, a swinging-60s nostalgia trip, was a small expansion pack for the original 1997 game. There will not be an updated version. You cannot really have high-speed car chases in a country that builds motorways more slowly than Rockstar makes games. And a British version of the in-game radio, on which B-list celebrities press budding female popstars for invitations to their 16th-birthday parties, would be a tough listen these days. Despite Britons’ sense of superiority over Americans, they cannot help but fantasise in American. “GTA” draws on this cognitive dissonance. Dan Houser, an English co-founder of Rockstar, once described “GTA” as a profile not of America but of “the America that was sold to the world”. Like Trollope’s book, the franchise is an outsider’s verdict on the country’s failure to live up to its glamorous image. Rockstar’s message is a confused one. America is both a botched experiment and a seductive, freewheeling fantasy. Vice City is a cesspit—and the best possible setting for a game based on

freedom-loving fun. Mr Houser personifies the contradiction. Having spent a career making fun of America, he now holds American citizenship. Why is Britain so good at selling America to Americans? International · International | The gang of four

A new axis of malice threatens the global order Russia, China, Iran and North Korea are co-operating like never before Nov 19th 2026 When Russia began using jet-powered Geran-4 drones against Ukraine in May, the battlespace began to shift in the Kremlin’s favour. They were much faster than Russia’s earlier, propeller-driven models, putting huge strain on Ukraine’s defences. But the new weapons signified a shift with global dimensions too. They were the product of intensifying, often secret, collaboration by an axis of autocracies with overlapping goals: Russia, China, Iran and North Korea. Once Ukrainian forces were able to inspect an intact Geran-4, they found it was based on Iran’s delta-winged Shahed suicide-drone, and upgraded with Russian-designed modules including a new warhead and optical navigation system (see diagram). The jet engine and anti-jamming antenna came from China, as did many other electronic parts. Internal markings showed that it

was made at Russia’s Alabuga drone factory in Tatarstan, where as many as 25,000 North Koreans are thought to work in a complex packed with Chinese industrial machinery. Collusion by this axis of malice goes far beyond the Geran-4. Its members are sharing military technology and expertise in ways that are altering the military balance of Europe, the Middle East and Asia. The network of manufacturers, traders, banks and ships that underpin collaboration is growing more sophisticated and resilient. And deepening economic ties are making the axis less susceptible to Western pressure. All that is making it harder to enforce UN and Western sanctions. One consequence is that Western and Ukrainian officials believe North Korea has obtained from Russia much of the drone technology in—and expertise in using—the Geran-4 and other weapons. That has been part of a pay-off for North Korea providing arms and some 16,000 troops to help fight Ukraine. Similar drone components and tactics are being used by Iran in the Middle East, too. Iran continues to send drone engineers to Russia, long after sharing initial Shahed designs, according to Ukrainian officials. “They are still going back and forth, exchanging knowledge,” says Vladyslav Vlasiuk, a Ukrainian presidential aide on sanctions policy.

China, meanwhile, has been regularly sending military officers and technicians to Russian facilities that produce and operate military drones. China’s capacity to manufacture drones is unrivalled. But it lacks combat experience: its last war was against Vietnam in 1979. So it is hoovering up Russian knowledge to improve its own drone designs and tactics, hoping to gain an edge in a potential conflict over Taiwan. Russia, Iran and North Korea, in turn, are likely to rely increasingly on China’s AI stack for cyber- attacks, disinformation campaigns and autonomous weapons. The axis is tilting the global nuclear balance as well. While China supplies Russia with machinery, drone parts and other “dual-use” items (with civilian and military applications), the Kremlin is helping it to build a missile- warning system and quieter nuclear-missile submarines, according to Western officials. Russia and China are also now enabling North Korea’s drive to become a fully fledged nuclear power. And covert support from Russia, China and North Korea has helped Iran to survive its conflict with America and Israel. This global realignment was the backdrop for a showy trilateral meeting on November 19th between Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping at a summit of Asia-Pacific powers in the Chinese city of Shenzhen. Some pundits have compared the meeting to the Yalta conference of 1945. But officials involved did not expect any breakthroughs. The axis, meanwhile, grows stronger. The nature of the axis coalition is debated by Western officials and scholars. Some see it as an emerging alliance of revisionist autocracies that threaten democracy worldwide. They liken it to the Eastern bloc in the cold war or the Axis alliance in the second world war. A book published this year by the Foundation for Defence of Democracies, a hawkish American think-tank, counted 600-plus instances of axis security co-operation in 2019-25, with a marked increase since 2022. Bradley Bowman, its lead author, argues that this co-operation in some ways surpasses that of the original Axis powers. It is “gaining momentum, making each adversary more formidable than they would be otherwise”. Sceptics see the grouping more as a temporary “axis of convenience” without common long-term goals or ironclad mutual-defence commitments.
