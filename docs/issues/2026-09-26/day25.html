Britain | Anywhere but here

Britain

Asylum-seekers are surprisingly even-handed. Some camps attract charities, which organise distracting activities. The Somali man who was moved to Napier barracks helped with translation and got involved with a local nature reserve. His medical and other basic needs were taken care of. By contrast, he had to scrounge furniture when he was later placed in a shared house. But he is young and outgoing. Others in the camp gave up hope, he says. Hotels began to house many more asylum-seekers from 2020 as a huge backlog built up (see chart 1). Everyone hates them. They tend to have the worst aspects of camps, such as minimal privacy and regimented meals, but few of the services. Sharing a bedroom with one stranger, as is normal in a hotel, can be as bad as sharing with many. An Afghan woman now living in Birmingham who spent several months in a hotel describes being put in a room with a woman who insisted on making phone calls in the middle of the night. Later she shared with a drug user. And hotels are costly. Last year the National Audit Office, a watchdog, noted that hotels housed one-third of asylum-seekers but accounted for three-quarters of spending on accommodation. Unlike barracks, hotels are easy targets for the far right.

With hotels unpopular and expensive, and a large network of camps little more than a Home Office fantasy, ever more of the burden of accommodating asylum-seekers is falling on residential streets. Almost 70,000 people, and rising, now live in “dispersal accommodation”, which usually means shared houses. The locations of the houses are changing rapidly. A decade ago asylum-seekers were usually placed in the poorer parts of ethnically diverse cities such as Birmingham, Glasgow, Liverpool and Middlesbrough. But some of the cities complained (in 2015 Liverpool’s mayor spoke of “asylum apartheid”) and the policy was changed. Today asylum-seekers are spread across the country (see map). In 2014 just one in four local authorities had any asylum-seekers in dispersal housing; today nine in ten do. In two years, South Kesteven in rural Lincolnshire has gone from three to 279.

Some rural asylum-seekers seem forgotten. One Yemeni migrant was placed in a Lincolnshire village, which he describes as “the end of the world”. The village shop was too expensive for him (asylum-seekers normally receive £49 a week to live on, or £10 if they are in a hotel and fed) and he could afford the bus to town only once a week. He struggled to talk to his English neighbours, who seemed uninterested, or his housemate, from Vietnam. “It makes you crazy,” he says. It took years for Britain’s cities to develop the clusters of lawyers, counsellors and other services that asylum-seekers rely on. Derby in the East Midlands became a “dispersal area” in 2002. Andrew Jackson of Community Action Derby, which co-ordinates charities in the city, says that a thick network of asylum organisations had emerged by about 2015. Rural areas may never acquire them. For now, asylum-seekers placed in small towns and villages seek help in places like Derby, when they can afford to visit them. Some small towns have turned hostile. The district of Nuneaton and Bedworth, near Birmingham, housed six asylum-seekers three years ago and had 314 at the last count in June. Last September councillors narrowly defeated a resolution that objected to the presence of any at all. Louise Calvey of Asylum Matters, a charity, says that asylum-seekers are far more

likely to experience hostility in places where they are new and noticeable because there are few other ethnic-minority people around. Other problems emerge when people try to settle in small towns and villages after being granted refugee status—as they sometimes do, partly because it can be easier to find public housing outside a city. English-language tuition in such places may be non-existent. And there is a reason small towns tend to have few ethnic minorities and migrants: they have few jobs. Refugees often struggle economically. Even after several years in Britain they are less likely to be in work than natives or other immigrants, and earn much less. Dispersing them away from cities is likely to make the problem worse. In their 2022 book “Streets of Gold”, the economists Ran Abramitzky and Leah Boustan showed that American immigrants succeed largely because they tend to land in places where jobs are plentiful. People who seek asylum in Britain are overwhelmingly happy to be there, and keen to get on with their lives. For all her housing problems, the Afghan woman in Birmingham says it has been much better than living under the Taliban. But Britons ought to have higher standards. Britain’s chancellor claims that the economy has turned a corner But is he right? Sep 24th 2026 John Healey is determined to be a sunny man. Britain’s new chancellor has learned from the mistakes of Rachel Reeves, his predecessor. In her first speech on the job she complained of inheriting “the worst set of circumstances since the second world war”. That left voters dejected. Mr Healey is more hopeful. In a recent speech he insisted that Britain is “turning a corner”. Its growth was “the fastest in the G7 in the first half of this year”. Even productivity is “finally ticking up” after stagnating since 2008. Is he right? The latest results do seem promising. But it is too soon to be confident of a lasting shift. The Office for National Statistics (ONS) reported that the economy grew by 1% in the first half of 2026. This beat the rest of the G7 group of big rich

countries (America was second with 0.9%). Some of Britain’s vitality can be attributed to technological progress: computing-related services grew by 8.9% in the seven months to the end of July, and output of computing and electrical equipment rose by 10.6%. Business investment grew strongly, too. But the results are probably flattered by seasonal quirks. The ONS smooths out fluctuations that recur each year—think Christmas shopping splurges— so that its statistics track underlying GDP. Since the pandemic, though, Britain’s seasonal patterns have shifted: more activity now falls in the early months, especially business investment. The ONS’s methodology has not yet fully caught up, which makes growth in the first half look better than it was. Forecasts for full-year growth suggest a less spectacular (albeit solid) performance. The Economist Intelligence Unit, our sister company, reckons that British GDP will expand by 1.2% in 2026, less than the 2.1% projection for America but still higher than Germany (1%) or France (0.6%). That implies a markedly weaker second half. This is partly the seasonal shenanigans happening in reverse, but it also reflects the impact of the Iran war. With oil regularly above $100 a barrel, more expensive energy will weigh heavily on the economy. In the long run Britain’s prosperity turns on how productive it is. The ONS recently admitted that output per hour had grown more quickly after 2008 than previously thought. But it still slowed considerably, causing wages to stagnate. The chancellor can be forgiven a jig at the thought that output per hour is rising again. Is it? The ONS’s headline statistics actually suggest that output per hour fell by an average of 0.2% a year between mid-2024 and mid-2026. But these results rely on workforce estimates from the unreliable Labour Force Survey, which has been plagued by low responses. Look at tax records, which even the ONS now recommends, and the signs are real. Using tax data, the Resolution Foundation, a think-tank, estimates that productivity grew by 1.1% a year between mid-2024 and mid-2026. This was a big improvement on the two prior years, when output per hour fell by 0.7% a year.

Advances in information technology account for nearly half of this recent growth. Those tempted to attribute it all to AI should be cautious. Computer- related jobs have long been the motor of British productivity growth; the industry’s contribution today is little higher than it was in the late 2010s. Some of the biggest changes in the past two years have come in sectors such as retail and hospitality (see chart). Labour politicians boasting about the improvement have kept quiet about its cause. Michael Saunders of Oxford Economics says output per worker has mostly risen because there are fewer workers, not higher output. The wholesale and retail sector has shed over 120,000 jobs in the past two years alone. Minimum-wage hikes and bigger employment taxes have driven up labour costs. Employers have substituted machines and tech for staff. This is good for the productivity statistics, but not for Labour’s promises to protect jobs. Mr Healey’s ultimate test is not whether GDP or productivity are growing but whether living standards are. Independent forecasts compiled by the Treasury suggest that, on average, real household disposable income will not grow at all this year. In his budget on October 28th the chancellor will need to set out not only his plans for growth but also how that growth will put more into people’s pockets. Labour’s re-election depends on it. ■
