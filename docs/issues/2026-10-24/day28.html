I know you.” Adam could barely hear her over the babble of the other captives. Militiamen had seized them as they fled the slaughter; now they were trapped in a disused school on the edge of the Sudanese desert. The woman was one of the locals let in to sell peanuts. Until she spoke, Adam had been in a kind of trance. When the city fell in October 2025, he was already weak from the months-long siege. All he’d been given to eat in the two weeks since were a few mouthfuls of flatbread a day. As his body shut down, the past and present blurred. He day-dreamed about the last days of el-Fasher: joking with friends at the hospital; his dog,

Bulldoze, padding behind him as he walked through the wards; the screams when the militia finally overran the city’s defences. The woman leaned closer, her headscarf slipping forwards. “You’re the doctor,” she repeated. “From Saudi hospital.” Suddenly Adam was alert. Fighters from the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), the militia that had captured him, were hunting down doctors. Hospital staff had evidence of the atrocities they had committed since they began fighting the Sudanese army in 2023; some doctors, like Adam, had spoken publicly about them. When Adam was first taken captive on the road out of el-Fasher, he’d been held under the scorching sun with thousands of others, and had watched RSF fighters search the crowds for people they considered enemies. They grabbed prisoners at random and shouted, “Where are the soldiers? Where are the doctors?” as they punched and kicked. The gunmen offered to let people go free if they informed on wanted men. Sometimes they snatched captives’ phones and searched them for incriminating material. Adam, who had left his phone at the hospital and shaved off his beard, managed to avoid discovery. Others weren’t so lucky. Several times he saw a fighter pull a man from the crowd and drag him behind the nearby trees. After a burst of shots, the fighter would come back alone. For all he knew the woman before him could be an RSF informant. “Sorry, you’re mistaken,” he told her politely. She moved on. A few days later she was back. She asked him again if he was the doctor, and he denied it a second time. He wasn’t sure he should have. He needed a friend. The only way out seemed to be to get a family member to pay a ransom to the RSF, but Adam had no way of contacting anyone.

In theory he could have escaped, as the school wasn’t heavily guarded. But the desert sands stretched all around him; he’d die of thirst without help. Someone might have mercy on him if he ran into the nearby village. But they might also report him to the RSF. Later, the woman tried a third time. “I know you,” she insisted. “You helped my sister.” Adam thought about his parents, who had escaped to safety, and his friends. He longed to see them again. With a deep sigh, he commended his life to God and leaned in close. “Yes,” he admitted. “I’m the doctor.” RSF forces stormed el-Fasher on October 26th 2025, briefly putting Sudan’s civil war on front pages. Humanitarians and diplomats had been warning for months that the RSF’s capture of the city, the last stronghold of the Sudanese army in the Darfur region, would lead to a bloodbath. The events that followed were only sparsely documented. Internet and phone signals were down, and those who survived often had their phones stolen. Researchers relied on satellite imagery to quantify the scale of the killing. The UN later concluded that it bore the “hallmarks of a genocide”. A year

after its fall to the RSF, investigators are still working out how a city that once housed a million people was emptied. El-Fasher has yet to gain the name recognition of other sites of modern-day massacres, such as Srebrenica. Adam had a happy childhood there, even during the previous civil war. A rebellion in Darfur broke out in 2003 when Adam was six, the result of decades of neglect by the corrupt, authoritarian government in Khartoum. When armed rebels, drawn from the region’s African ethnic groups, attacked military bases, the Arab-dominated regime in the capital retaliated against the rebels’ communities. Warplanes bombed villages, burning families alive. The Janjaweed, a feared Arab militia, were sent in. They looted, gang-raped women, and bludgeoned children to death. But these horrors impinged only tangentially on Adam’s life in el-Fasher. For centuries, the city had been a hub on trans-Saharan caravan routes linking north, central and east Africa, and it retained its cosmopolitan roots. Children from different African ethnic groups as well as pupils with Arab roots studied together at Adam’s primary school. His classmates sometimes relayed gossip they’d overheard about bands of marauders in the countryside, but Adam was more interested in talking about football and anime series, like “Detective Conan”.

Eventually a series of truces, together with the deployment of an armed peacekeeping force, tamped down the violence in Darfur. As Adam got older, he noticed things he’d overlooked as a young child: the refugee camp on the edge of the city; the portraits of the president, Omar al-Bashir, looking down from the walls of shops; the fact that no one would talk about politics in public. For him and his family at least, life was good. His parents both had decent jobs, and he and his two sisters grew up in a spacious house with a garden. Even as a child, Adam knew that he wanted to help people, so he studied hard in science in the hope of one day becoming a doctor. On special occasions, the family went out for dinner at shisha bars illuminated by lights strung between the trees like glowing cobwebs. Sometimes Adam’s father took him for coffee in el-Fasher’s main market, where the shops glittered with bangles made from gold mined in south Darfur. By the time Adam started studying medicine at el-Fasher university in 2014, the economy was crumbling. The Bashir regime’s dysfunction had helped create a surge in food and fuel prices. Critics were disappearing. Protesters around the country began demanding Bashir’s resignation; Adam helped organise demonstrations at the university. He and his fellow students were teargassed, beaten and sometimes jailed. In April 2019, after months of popular revolt, Bashir’s security chiefs forced him to step down. A fledgling transitional government took charge with the backing of the two main powerbrokers in the country: the Sudanese army and the RSF, the official paramilitary organisation that had emerged from the Janjaweed. For the first time in Adam’s life, peace and democracy seemed within reach. Adam and his fellow students would stay up late chatting about their dreams for a new country. Then in 2021, the army and the RSF overthrew the civilian government in a coup. Adam was deep in his studies by then, and struggled to juggle exam preparation with participating in pro-democracy protests.
