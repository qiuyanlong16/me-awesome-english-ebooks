His proud father hosted a barbeque for their family and friends; guests sipped sweet, sticky glasses of ruby-red hibiscus juice while frankincense smoke curled through the evening air. Afterwards Adam and the other graduates zoomed around town, cheering loudly and hooting their car horns. The next week he started work at el-Fasher’s teaching hospital. Later, he’d marvel at how cosseted he was there. One of the hospital’s 30 consultants was always there to give him advice. When he had to tell a young woman she had cancer, older staff counselled him on how to break the news gently. On the morning of April 15th 2023 Adam was at home getting ready for a shift when his phone buzzed and he looked down. He didn’t know it at the time, but it was the moment his old life ended. Wassim, a 25-year-old laboratory technician who would later become one of Adam’s closest friends, was at the teaching hospital when the news broke. He had recently graduated, and had brought his CV to the hospital seeking an internship. As he waited he heard the crack of gunshots outside, but assumed it was just another protest being dispersed. Within half an hour, bloodied civilians began stumbling in. Hospital staff triaged them while

shouting out the updates flashing on their phones. Gunmen were fighting near the military barracks a few streets away. The RSF and the army were at war. Staff and patients fled the building. Two other hospitals caught up in the fighting also had to close. Along with other doctors, nurses and support staff, Adam made his way to South hospital, then the only one operating. The next few days were chaos, with casualties piling up and the hospital stretched beyond its limits. Fortunately, the Sudanese are used to handling crises without any help from the state. The network of volunteers that had helped organise pro-democracy demonstrations became an emergency- response force: mending, scrubbing and scrounging for supplies to keep the hospital working. Wassim, his internship application long forgotten, joined in. One of the most prominent figures in the network was Abdullah, a graduate in management studies. Abdullah and Adam were university friends, and had sometimes marched in the same protests. Abdullah’s easy manner and confidence meant he was often called on to give speeches at community events, and he was well-known even before the war. Once it started, he got to work asking people for money and medicine on behalf of the hospital. Sometimes there was a pretty young woman at the hospital, who caught Abdullah’s attention. Aisha, an economics graduate, had been visiting an ailing relative when the war broke out and then pitched in to assist with the volunteer effort. She helped Abdullah look after a mentally ill man who had been injured, and later they distributed medicines together. Abdullah was drawn to her spirit and grit.

Adam, too, threw himself into the collective effort, sometimes joining Wassim for night-time raids on abandoned hospitals in search of supplies. He decided he was most needed at the Saudi maternity hospital (or “Saudi”, as everyone called it), which had reopened two weeks after the start of the war. Other doctors and volunteers began splitting their time between Saudi and South. For a few months it looked as though el-Fasher might be spared the worst of the conflict ravaging other parts of Sudan. The governor of Darfur, Minni Minnawi, was a former rebel leader. His fighters had been at war with both the Sudanese army and the RSF’s predecessor, the Janjaweed, for years, and trusted neither. Shortly after the fighting broke out, Minnawi declared his forces’ neutrality. Instead of becoming a battle site, el-Fasher was divided into zones of influence. Minnawi’s men held a buffer zone in the centre. The RSF held the east. The Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) held the west and parts of the south, where South and Saudi hospitals were located. Like most people, Adam and Wassim knew fighters in all three groups. The city was tense, but it functioned.

Then in November 2023, Minnawi announced that he was going to fight alongside the Sudanese armed forces, citing RSF atrocities. Panic gripped el- Fasher. Families strapped bulging suitcases onto cars and fled. One night Saudi’s senior medical director called Adam. They’d worked closely together, and he wanted to tell Adam himself that he was leaving. Then came the kicker: he asked Adam to take over his responsibilities, alongside two other people. Saudi had a staff of 400, and was the only specialist maternity hospital in Darfur, a region of 12m people. Adam was ten months out of medical school. That night he lay awake for hours, his mind churning. Would the older staff accept him? Where should he start? He began compiling lists of available staff in his head. He awoke feeling calmer. There was no point trying to fake authority. He had to earn trust by being humble: cleaning the floors himself and leaning on his more experienced colleagues. Rather than make a big speech, he met staff individually. “You are the real leaders of the hospital,” he told them. He was particularly reliant on the hospital’s middle-aged administrative director, Mahasin, who was a surrogate mother to the younger staff. She ticked them off over behaviour she considered risky, such as leaving the hospital for coffee or parties, and cooked up enormous vats of food for them.

The hospital was Adam’s home now. He moved in with some essential belongings, including Bulldoze. A few months later fighting reached the neighbourhood where his family lived. His parents and sisters decided to flee, packing only a few bags of clothes and Adam’s degree certificate. He escorted them to the city gates in the morning, hugged them, then went back to work. He found it hard to concentrate on anything until they called that evening to say they had made it out safely. As the RSF seized town after town in Darfur, reports of atrocities mounted. Adam’s mother begged him to flee himself. In November the RSF captured el-Geneina, a city 300km west of el-Fasher. Civilians from the tribes fighting the RSF were executed; some were tortured to death. Those who tried to escape were shot. If they did that to el-Geneina, his mother said, what did he think the RSF would do in el-Fasher, the centre of resistance? After several of these entreaties, Adam snapped. He told his mother he was already dead. “Consider that I have given my life to this city,” he said. “Consider me a martyr.” They spoke most days, but she never brought up the subject of him leaving again.

The war sometimes got to Adam. He took care to hide black moods from his colleagues. Often the only place he could find solitude was in the operating room, where he’d lean against the walls and brood in the antiseptic dark. Small victories buoyed him, such as the time his team was able to save a baby who had been shot in its mother’s womb. But the injuries were becoming worse as both sides’ weapons got more powerful. When the war first broke out, Adam and his colleagues had mainly treated people for bullet wounds. But then both sides obtained more sophisticated equipment, including drones. Though the RSF tried to keep their foreign benefactors a secret, an overwhelming body of evidence pointed to the UAE (the UAE denies the accusation). By the summer of 2024, people were arriving at Saudi eviscerated, or with limbs sliced off. One day in June 2024 about 50 wounded people arrived at Saudi within half an hour. Many were women and children with gaping shrapnel wounds. Adam ran to help with triage when someone yelled: “South hospital has been overrun by the RSF!” He refused to believe it at first. So many of his friends were working there. Then he froze as he noticed how dirty and tired the new arrivals looked: they had run the 5km distance between the two hospitals. The rumour was true. Over the next few days, staff from South hospital limped in. Many had scaled the hospital walls under fire to escape. Among them was the director- general of the facility, who had been beaten, stabbed in the face and left for dead.

The staff didn’t know what happened after they fled, but they found out a few days later when a team was briefly able to re-enter. Some patients were shot in their beds; the bodies of their relatives lay sprawled in the lobby. A handful of survivors said the RSF had rounded up men of military age. Saudi was now the last functioning hospital in the city. They got used to the sounds of drones overhead. The smaller ones whined; the bigger, military-grade ones sounded like small planes. Sometimes quadrocopters hovered for hours, blades whirring, before releasing a bomb. Adam managed to get hold of some shipping containers, and the team buried them to use as bomb shelters. But there were no sirens to alert them to attacks, and often people didn’t make it to the shelter in time. Amna, a pharmacist with a penchant for romance novels who had joined them after escaping South hospital, was killed in a direct hit on Saudi’s pharmacy. Soon Adam gave up trying to run to the shelter when the bombs fell. Ignoring Mahasin’s scolding, he would enjoy his coffee in the sunshine instead, with Bulldoze by his side. “No one cared any more,” Adam recalled. “You would hear a bomb hit the hospital and just shrug.”
