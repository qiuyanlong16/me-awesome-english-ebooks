One of the RSF soldiers beckoned to Aisha. Abdullah stepped forward instead, meekly explaining that his wife was pregnant. To his surprise, the RSF soldier waved them through. “Go with my best wishes,” he told them. Abdullah looked the man in the face, astonished. It was the first time he’d seen evidence of humanity in an RSF fighter. He never forgot it. That evening Adam and the remaining crew talked about whether they should follow Abdullah and Aisha out of el-Fasher. They were determined to live or die together, so they held a vote. Adam, like the majority of them, voted to stay. It felt appalling to leave the patients to the RSF. But he sensed it would all be over soon no matter what he did. That night, as explosions shook the building, he walked through the hospital in the dark, saying a silent goodbye. He patted Bulldoze. Ali, one of the last pharmacists, lay awake listening to the RSF artillery. In the early hours he fetched the others and told them they should vote again on leaving. “It’s the end,” he said. Reluctantly, Adam agreed. It was shortly before sunrise. They had no plan—they just ran outside. All Adam took with him was a bottle of water and his Rose Tobacco. Terrified families filled the streets; mothers carried crying children. A few were riding donkeys but most were on foot. They were heading west, towards the city’s last collapsing defence.

Behind them, giddy RSF fighters roared in from the south, east and north, looting and shooting. Crowds of people seeking refuge at the university were strafed and mortared. Families were forced to lie down at gunpoint and crushed with armoured vehicles. Later, videos would emerge of an RSF commander gleefully murdering groups of unarmed people. He boasted that he had personally killed more than 2,000. Adam and the rest of the hospital workers joined a flood of people heading west, but the RSF seized them on the road. In the chaos, Adam’s friends were put on trucks while he was marched to the abandoned school. For the first time since the siege began, he and his colleagues were separated. Wassim, Ali and Yasir were imprisoned in the yard of the abandoned Children’s hospital, along with thousands of others. For three days, there was virtually no water. Some prisoners died of dehydration. When a commander eventually brought in a tank, dying men swarmed towards it, trampling each other. A fighter shot into the crowd, killing three people. Maddened by thirst, the prisoners broke into the septic tank and began to scoop up the fetid water with their hands. There was another cholera outbreak and diarrhoea coated the ground. The RSF fighters, terrified the

disease would spread, appealed for doctors to come forward from the crowd. An official said there had been orders from the top not to kill people with medical skills. The Saudi team worried it was a trap. On the other hand, they were as good as dead anyway. Eventually, one by one, they pitched in, isolating cholera patients with a barricade of broken chairs and branches, and treating them as best they could with the medicines they were given. Over the course of the next ten days they managed to contain the outbreak, though not before 200 people had died. Afterwards, the RSF took them back to Saudi. The militia’s leaders seemed to want the hospital working again. In the courtyard the team found a small boy crying. He whispered to them that his mother and father, both patients, had been killed by RSF gunmen just after the doctors had left. The UN later said around 460 people were shot dead when the hospital was taken—mostly patients in their beds. A few weeks later Wassim, Ali and Yasir all managed to escape, reaching the border with Chad via a circuitous route of small villages. They worried all the time about what had happened to Adam.

Far away, in the darkened schoolroom on the edge of the desert, Adam was listening to the peanut-seller explain how she would help him escape. Tell the guards you need to relieve yourself, she said, then meet me at the back of the building. He did as she said, walking unsteadily into the glaring sun. The woman guided him surreptitiously to her home, where he hid for 20 days. Adam moved from house to house, mostly at night. Each time a person hid him, he’d ask them to try to find someone on the path ahead who might help. Sometimes a friendly driver would give him a lift. It took him a month to reach Khartoum. He was desperate to find out what had happened to his friends, but was still afraid that the RSF would track him down. But now we are forgotten.” Every time he connects his phone to the internet it is flooded with messages from desperate colleagues: asking for medicine, a job or money. It overwhelms him. But he can’t switch off. He is a helper. If he doesn’t help, who is he? In a strange way, he said, he and the others felt nostalgia for the siege. Not for the suffering, but for the purpose and unity they had back then, and the family they became.

The AI industry’s gigantic debts are making investors nervous Who will be left to pick up the bill in the event of a crash? Oct 22nd 2026 Credit markets are coming for CoreWeave. The price of a five-year bond issued by the “neocloud”, which rents out data-centre capacity for artificial- intelligence models, has fallen by 16% since July. The bond’s yield is now 15%—a level associated with companies in financial distress—up from 10% in July. The price of credit-default swaps offering insurance against a CoreWeave default implies that the probability of the firm being unable to service its debts in the next five years has risen above 60% (see chart 1). Investors increasingly worry that the companies at the heart of the AI boom will be unable to generate the cashflows necessary to service their vast debts. These concerns have spilled over from credit markets to cause a wobble in the stock market, too: having fallen sharply, the tech-heavy

NASDAQ index is now back to where it was in mid-May. Investors are pricing in a debt stock that is getting bigger, more opaque—and increasingly risky. It is not just CoreWeave causing furrowed brows. Yields on the bonds of rival neoclouds, including Nebius and TeraWulf, are also rising. On October 19th the public listing of Elice, a South Korean competitor, turned sour as its share price plunged by a third in a day. Three others have delayed or cancelled their own listings in the past month. These businesses live on debt. Of the ten listed neoclouds, only two are profitable. The combined borrowings of this group, at $100bn, are nine times its annual revenues. Of that debt, $57bn-worth has been issued this year. Moreover, those sums are but a small part of the fast-growing stock of AI- related debt. Oracle, a software pedlar turned cloud provider, has now racked up $169bn of borrowings, with $25bn-worth issued this year. SoftBank, a Japanese conglomerate, has similar ambitions, and has taken out about as much since the start of January. Elon Musk’s SpaceX, for which revenue is rocketing thanks mostly to its fast-growing cloud business, has issued $65bn of debt this year.

Then there are the leading “hyperscalers”—Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta—which have borrowed $214bn between them in 2026, up from $83bn in all of last year. An additional $79bn of data-centre debt has been issued by special-purpose vehicles (SPVs), most of which are joint ventures between cloud providers and financial institutions. On top of this is the $84bn borrowed by chipmakers such as Nvidia and others in the semiconductor supply chain. Add the amount issued by other companies providing data-centre gear and America’s electricity utilities, which are also having to invest to keep up with the AI boom, and the total borrowed so far this year rises to nearly $850bn—roughly four times the amount the same group of companies issued in 2023 (see chart 2). Together their balance- sheets carry more than $3trn of debt. Not long ago the AI boom was financed by the cashflows of the hyperscalers. But investment has grown beyond what can be paid for by their other businesses, and far beyond what consumers and enterprises are so far spending on AI. JPMorgan Chase, a bank, expects around $5.5trn in capital spending between now and 2030 to support the technology’s growth, with three-quarters of that funded by debt. Borrowers are turning to imaginative financing strategies, and tapping all manner of lenders.
