It belongs to Britannia Hotels, Britain’s largest privately owned hotel chain, with 64 hotels and over 10,000 rooms. Britannia has finished last in 12 consecutive surveys of big hotel chains by Which? magazine. Yet many of those years coincided with healthy profits. Now Britannia is changing course. Its 88-year-old founder, Alex Langsam, has stepped back and a four- person management board plans “immediate and rolling upgrades” to rejuvenate its hotels and reputation. Why the shift? It is a tricky time for British hotels. Wages, business rates and energy costs are rising—and customers are increasingly value-conscious. Britannia is positioned as a budget operator, with an average room price (£84 in 2025, according to Which?) higher than Travelodge’s (£69) but lower than Premier Inn’s (£94). Those chains offer consistency in standardised hotels, many purpose-built and leased. Britannia has an owner-operator model. Under Mr Langsam it accumulated an eclectic estate of grand old buildings. He once described his company as merely the “custodians for a period of time” of such heritage assets. For years its expanding estate delivered erratic returns. Then its fortunes improved. Pre-tax profits averaged nearly £20m in the three years before covid-19, despite dismal survey ratings. This coincided with an important new customer: the government. Hoteliers could earn “much more” from housing asylum-seekers, says Philippa Goldstein of Knight Frank, a consultancy. Government spending on asylum hotels surged to more than £2bn a year after covid. Britannia’s pre-tax profits hit a record £39m in 2023; reports suggested that at least 17 of its hotels were block-booked on the government’s behalf. But as the number of asylum-seekers in hotels has fallen from over 56,000 to around 16,000, its annual profits have dipped to £27m. One hotel, Daresbury Park (“located just off Junction 11 of the M56”), featured prominently in the 2025 Runcorn and Helsby by-election. During the campaign the Home Office announced that its asylum contract would end; the anti-immigration Reform UK won by six votes anyway. Since then Britannia has given the hotel a multi-million-pound glow-up. On a recent visit, an £84 room was smart and the evening bar lively, especially for an out-of-town business park in Cheshire. Britannia calls

Daresbury Park a “prime example” of what its investment can achieve. The hope is that its refurbished hotels will attract more paying customers as government contracts recede. That is a work in progress. Back at the Adelphi, a dated bedroom came with a pristine new bathroom, although the shower prevented the door opening beyond half way. Several coach parties of older guests added to the throwback atmosphere. For Britannia preserves something besides old buildings: a tradition of communal British holiday-making. At coastal properties this year Britannia introduced a seaside-entertainment programme, with bingo, quizzes and live shows. Some of Britain’s most high-profile old buildings have recently been repurposed as luxury hotels under international hospitality groups. In London the Old War Office is now a Raffles hotel; Admiralty Arch’s conversion into a Waldorf Astoria is nearly complete. Both are advertising rooms at upwards of £800 a night. A stay at the Adelphi, a Liverpool landmark owned by a British company, set The Economist back £53.40. Britain’s elite may soon become harder to spot “Who’s Who”, a reference book that tracks them, is being sold Oct 22nd 2026 “THATCHER, Baroness” liked “music, reading”. “CHAMBERLAIN, Col. Sir Neville”, as an earlier volume of “Who’s Who” recorded, enjoyed “shooting”. “STALIN, Joseph” and “HITLER, Adolf” did not deign to send in their recreations (unlike “MUSSOLINI, Benito”, who liked “violin, horse- riding, fencing”). “CHURCHILL, Rt. Hon. Winston” did not find time to record his, either. After war broke out, though, he did find time to personally intervene and ensure that “Who’s Who” would still be printed despite paper shortages. It was, he considered, of “national importance”. And it used to be. First published in 1849, “Who’s Who” is the world’s oldest biographical reference annual, one of its most influential and, arguably, its oddest. Its pillar-box red volumes were conceived to catalogue a certain sort of British elite and the occasional foreigner (the first entry

listed in 1849 was “our Beloved SOVEREIGN”, Queen Victoria). But its influence, much like that of its elite, spread, and soon it had colonised other realms. “Who’s Who” was followed by “Wer ist wer?” for Germany, “Hvem er hvem?” for Norway and even “Who Was Who” for the afterlife. Leon Trotsky was included in the 1938 "Who’s Who”. In the current edition John McDonnell, a former Labour MP, lists his recreations as “fermenting the overthrow of capitalism”—with spelling, evidently, being less of an enthusiasm. Now Bloomsbury, which publishes “Who’s Who”, has announced that it is selling it. It is uncertain who the buyer will be, or indeed whether it will continue to be printed. This matters. “Who’s Who” has lasted for so long, and gathered so much data—179 editions and over 125,000 entries—that it offers less mere anthology than anthropology. Past editions provide an unrivalled data source on that elusive species, the English elite. They list everything from habitats (each entry gives “Address” and “Clubs”) to hobbies (“Recreations” were added in 1897) and even elite humour: the comedian John Cleese listed his pastimes as “gluttony, sloth”. How the “Who” are selected is complicated. Broadly speaking, entrants are split into two categories. One is bigwigs you mostly haven’t heard of (MPs, KCs, judges, peers, other types who wear tights), who gain automatic admission by virtue of their roles. The other is bigwigs you may know (actors, writers, business figures), whose entry is voted on by a board of advisers. Though quite who chooses the choosers is unclear: to stop bribery and corruption it is, says Sam Friedman, a professor of sociology at the LSE, “shrouded in secrecy”. Its outcomes, by contrast, are illuminating. The elite of “Who’s Who” are, as Professor Friedman has shown, notably more male (over 80% are men) and pale (almost 97% are white) than Britain as a whole. They are also more likely to live in London (almost 36%, versus 13% for the average Briton), more likely to be privately educated (47% versus 6%) and far likelier to have gone to Oxbridge (35% versus 1%). Who is who in Britain feels rather similar to who was who—and both are largely who you would expect. In the 1850s a quarter of entrants listed “hunting” as a hobby. One later one liked hunting “tusker elephants” and another “hunting the Philistines”.

What for socialists is cause for handwringing is, for historians, cause for celebration. Such an archive is “absolute gold dust”, says Sir Tristram Hunt, director of the V&A museum. Even in an era of online oversharing “Who’s Who”, in its longevity, comparability and sheer triviality, offers the historian information that he says is “very, very hard to find elsewhere”. (Such as, perhaps, the fact that “HUNT, Tristram” enjoys “fresh-water swimming, ceramics [and] book-browsing”.) Some of the most telling details are incidental. Early volumes have telegram addresses, car number plates and a habit of referring to female writers as “authoress”. Best of all it is still in print, for its volumes speak volumes. Whereas written history is, says Sir Tristram, “as much about the present as the past”, “Who’s Who” offers a yesterday unmediated by today. Open the 1938 volume and those entries—“HITLER, Adolf”, “STALIN, Joseph”, “CHURCHILL, Winston”—feel eerie in their unknowing; a cast list for a play that is yet to be played whose ending is yet to be written. “Who’s Who” can feel like a monument to an unchanging elite. But it shows Britain’s elites are changing: fewer list hunting as a hobby; now they like pets and displaying an awkward irreverence towards things like “Who’s Who”. (Several list “sex” as a recreation.) They are, probably, less likely to display a copy of “Who’s Who” at home. Britain’s elite will not disappear but it may, thinks Professor Friedman, be harder to spot if this “important window” is lost. Like most monuments to magnificence, however, “Who’s Who” is also an Ozymandias-like monument to obsolescence. Pick up an old volume today and what strikes you most is not the entrants’ authority but their anonymity. Mockery keeps Britain’s politicians honest. (Almost) The ancient art of the parliamentary sketch is thriving in the digital era Oct 22nd 2026 “The sluice gates of mediocrity opened. Before long the audience were up to their beaks in it, like chickens in a flash flood.” The Daily Mail’s verdict on a recent speech by Lucy Powell, Britain’s education secretary, was not kind. Nor was the Times’s on Sir Ed Davey, leader of the Liberal Democrats, who “looked and sounded, as he always does, like a smiley rural GP doling out cod-liver oil to old ladies” during his party-conference speech in September. Both got off lightly compared with the Conservatives’ shadow chancellor. “Even 20 minutes of Andrew Griffith can rip your soul in half,” the Guardian wrote in October. “Griffith proceeded to give the worst speech ever heard. Not just in content but in delivery. It was like watching a less sentient version of the PG Tips monkey.”

Such indelicate sentiments are common on social media, but in most countries they rarely grace the pages of mainstream newspapers. On Fleet Street, however, they are positively encouraged. For that you can thank the ancient, still-thriving art of the British parliamentary sketch. When John Healey, the chancellor, delivers his first budget on October 28th, he will care most about the responses of MPs, voters and the bond market. But he will probably keep half an eye on the sketches, too, since a well- turned phrase in a sketch can dog a politician for the rest of their career. Theresa May, while prime minister, never managed to escape the nickname of “Maybot”, attached to her by the Guardian’s John Crace after a particularly robotic set of interviews about Brexit. A sketch has nothing to do with drawing. Rather, it is a comic but factual report of a political news event—usually parliamentary proceedings, a speech or an interview. It recounts what was said and what the sketch writer thought of it, along with plenty of jokes at the politicians’ expense. Four daily newspapers and two magazines employ sketch writers; in contrast, all British media outlets between them now employ only one trade-union correspondent. The practice began in the early 19th century and every sketch writer dreams of reaching the heights of their greatest predecessor, Charles Dickens, who wrote for the Mirror of Parliament.
