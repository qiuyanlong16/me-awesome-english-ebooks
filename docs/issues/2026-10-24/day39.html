Advocates of “metascience”, the study of scientific discovery, have spent years pointing out the foibles of the system. Lately they have been putting their ideas into practice. Private institutions, many of which were founded within the past five years, say they are building the future of science. The history of American innovation suggests they may be right. Private science is nothing new. Many of the most exciting American discoveries of the 20th century were not dreamt up on university campuses, but within corporate research institutes. Scientists at Bell Labs (the research arm of AT&T), Xerox PARC (the copy-machine maker’s lab) and IBM Research came up with such marvels as the transistor, and won a handful of Nobel prizes in so doing. Yet for all their successes, private labs failed to justify their upkeep. A paper published in 2004 by William Nordhaus, an economist at Yale University, estimated that, in the post-war period and across the whole economy, just 2.2% of value from innovation flowed to firms. In response, companies transitioned to “R&D by M&A,” buying startups spun out from universities rather than funding their own labs. Corporate labs’ retreat led basic research to become much more reliant on government-backed efforts. In the view of private-science advocates, that is lamentable. The concentration of science funding means fewer innovative ideas can benefit society at large, says Caleb Watney of the Institute for Progress, a think-tank. To wit, a study of 100,000 National Institutes of Health (NIH) grant applications, conducted in 2025 by Pierre Azoulay of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Wesley Greenblatt of Harvard Medical School, found that projects which took out-of-consensus approaches were less likely to have their funding renewed. Another entrant to the world of private science is Episteme, a company launched in 2025 by Sam Altman of OpenAI and Louis Andre, then a little- known researcher. Rather than demanding pre-set targets or papers, Episteme hopes to give its lead researchers near-total freedom. While the select few it funds often have an approach in mind to solving their chosen problem, Episteme offers them the flexibility to adapt. “If an idea fails, which in science is something that happens very often, you should be able to pivot and try a different angle,” says Mr Andre. The Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) intends for that to change. On October 20th its Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission lifted off from Tanegashima, an island in southern Japan. The probe will spend the next year travelling to Mars, where it aims to land on Phobos, the larger of the planet’s two moons, collect at least ten grams of rock and dust, and return the haul to Earth in 2031. Planetary scientists hope the mission will resolve a long-standing puzzle: how Mars’s moons were formed—and, in the process, reveal much about the planet itself. Phobos and Mars’s other moon, Deimos, are small, respectively about 22km and 12km across, and look like lumpy potatoes. That might suggest they are asteroids that were captured by Mars’s gravitational field after straying too

close. Captured asteroids, however, usually have tilted, elliptical orbits, whereas both moons follow nearly circular paths around the planet’s equator. That hints at another possible origin: a giant collision that threw debris into orbit that later gathered into moons. JAXA has spent over a decade developing MMX, working with the space agencies of America, Europe and Australia. The mission builds on Japan’s record of returning samples from space. Its Hayabusa mission collected small amounts of grains from Itokawa, an asteroid which comes close to Earth, and brought them home in 2010. Its successor, Hayabusa2, brought back five grams of material—well above the mission’s target—from Ryugu, another near-Earth asteroid, in 2020. The samples proved rich in water- bearing molecules and organic compounds, offering clues to how asteroids may have carried the ingredients for life to Earth. MMX’s mission is more demanding. Phobos’s surface gravity is roughly one-2,000th of Earth’s, but it is 40 times greater than Ryugu’s. That is strong enough to pose a crash risk for a delicate spacecraft, explains Erik Asphaug, a planetary scientist at the University of Arizona. The Hayabusa missions also followed a “touch-and-go” method in which the spacecraft briefly kissed the asteroids. In contrast, MMX will land on Phobos and extract samples from beneath its surface. And MMX’s makers want to do a lot more during its three years orbiting Mars. An instrument called MEGANE (pronounced “meh-gah-neh”, like the Japanese word for spectacles), developed by NASA, will measure gamma rays and neutrons given off when cosmic rays hit Phobos, thus revealing its chemistry. A small European rover will explore its surface. After departing Phobos, MMX will conduct fly-bys of Deimos to observe it, too, up close. If the samples make it safely back to Earth, scientists reckon they might settle which of the two origin stories is true. If Phobos is a captured asteroid, its rocks may resemble meteorites from elsewhere in the solar system. If it instead formed after a giant impact, it will contain melt fragments generated by intense heating.
