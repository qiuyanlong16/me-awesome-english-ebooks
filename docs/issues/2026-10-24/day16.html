A trip to Minas Gerais, the country’s second-most populous state, shows the hill Lula has to climb. It is a microcosm of Brazil: its arid north borders the country’s poor north-east, Lula’s heartland. To its south and west, agribusiness and industry reign supreme and voters lean right. No president has ever won without taking Minas. Though Lula carried the state in 2022, he lost it by five percentage points to Mr Bolsonaro on October 4th. In Monte Sião, a town of 25,000 people, some 2,000 tiny firms churn out knitwear from the ground floors of the owners’ houses. Residents complain about the rising cost of living. In the backroom of a small firm, five seamstresses are adding the finishing touches to dresses. “We pay so much in taxes, taxes, taxes,” complains one. “We have no money left over at the end of the month—we earn just to survive.” Her colleague chimes in: “It’s called the Workers’ Party, but the worker gets nothing!” Their boss blames crushing interest rates for pushing up his debt. Fatigue with Lula’s Workers’ Party (PT), which has been in power for 18 of the past 24 years, is common in these parts. In Poços de Caldas, a town nearby that is known for its thermal baths and abundant deposits of rare earths, voters are disheartened. “Lula said he would give everyone picanha (rump steak), but today even eggs cost a lot,” says a 45-year-old bricklayer, referring to a promise Lula made on the campaign trail in 2022, that he

Latin America is embracing Israel New right-wing leaders are rushing to upgrade ties Oct 22nd 2026 “Something remarkable is happening in Latin America”, grinned Binyamin Netanyahu, Israel’s embattled prime minister, on October 14th. “It’s all coming back! The great friendship.” He was speaking following the opening of Colombia’s new embassy in Jerusalem. Colombia has become the fourth —and so far the most populous—Latin American country to recognise the holy city as Israel’s capital. It is unlikely to be the last. Israel has grown more isolated as a result of the war in Gaza. Yet conservative leaders in Latin America—many of them newly elected—are dramatically stepping up ties. Javier Milei, Argentina’s president, is the keenest. Earlier this year he told The Economist he thought Mr Netanyahu was a “superstar”. He himself has claimed, oddly, to be the “most Zionist president in the world”. In April Mr Milei announced the “Isaac Accords”, a

vague framework for closer engagement between Israel and the region. A new direct flight between Buenos Aires and Tel Aviv launches next month. Flávio Bolsonaro, the frontrunner in Brazil’s presidential election, has vowed to relocate his country’s embassy from Tel Aviv to Jerusalem if he wins on October 25th. He expressed his “full support” for Israeli settlers during a recent visit to the West Bank; both he and Mr Milei have prayed before the Western Wall. As well as moving its embassy, Colombia in August became the only country other than the United States to say it supported Israel’s claim to the Golan Heights. “We have never paid so much attention to our relationship with Israel,” says Sandra Borda of the University of the Andes in Bogotá. Rodrigo Paz in Bolivia, José Antonio Kast in Chile and Nasry Asfura in Honduras have all bolstered diplomatic ties that their predecessors had downgraded in response to the bloodshed in Gaza. Delcy Rodríguez, the interim president of Venezuela, has softened the chavista regime’s longstanding hostility towards Israel. After big earthquakes near Caracas in June she accepted Israel’s offer to send rescue teams and resumed consular ties. “Latin America is becoming a blue continent,” says Marcos Peckel of the Confederation of Jewish Communities in Colombia, referring to Israel’s blue-and-white flag. Some of the region’s leaders are motivated by strong personal convictions. Mr Milei is known to study the Torah with a rabbi. When The Economist visited his office in August, a Hebrew text sat on his desk. In a speech to Israel’s parliament in 2025, he vowed to “defend Western culture”. Yet political calculations are plainly central, too. Moving closer to Israel is one way for the new right in Latin America to curry favour with America’s Republican Party, and with Donald Trump. The political costs are relatively low, says Christopher Sabatini of Chatham House, a think-tank in London. A survey by Pew, a pollster, finds that a little over half of Argentines, Brazilians, Colombians and Peruvians have an unfavourable view of Israel—much lower than the 69% reported in Britain and 78% in Spain. In most of Latin America, questions related to Israel and Palestine matter far less to voters than fighting crime and boosting the economy.
