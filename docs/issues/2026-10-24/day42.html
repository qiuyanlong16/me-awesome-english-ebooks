Leadership lessons from Napoleon How the French emperor’s management style ultimately led to his fall Oct 22nd 2026 What management tips can you learn from conquerors? Put aside oppression, and there are lessons about delegation, motivation, recruitment and team building. Alexander the Great refused to drink water before battle to stand in solidarity with his men. Caesar marched on foot alongside his men, even swimming across rivers, to demonstrate his derring-do. When his army wobbled, he would fling himself in the way of his fleeing men, “even catching them by the throat and forcing them to face the enemy”, according to Suetonius, a Roman historian. Diocletian divided the Roman Empire into four, and shared power while keeping a firm central grip. In 2014 Andrew Roberts, a prolific British historian and member of the House of Lords, published an excellent biography of Napoleon and his boldness on the battlefield. In a new book he examines Napoleon’s marshal

system. The day after he proclaimed himself emperor of France in 1804, Napoleon appointed 18 “marshals of the empire”, later increasing the tally to 26. They were to be “the swords around Napoleon’s throne”, the army’s most venerated figures and the pinnacle of French society. Some recruits came from the nobility; others were craftsmen’s sons. They were indulged with fortunes, land and even marriages into the imperial family. Each was given a deep-blue velvet high-collared uniform trimmed with golden embroidery, complete with a red silk sash, and a baton inscribed with the gold imperial eagles. Napoleon made six of them princes and crowned two kings. The marshals’ elite leadership team was built to carry out Napoleon’s orders and bring glory to France by executing his new theory of warfare, with fewer set-piece sieges and more agile manoeuvres, thanks to an army that lived off the land. But Napoleon also devised an efficient modern management system, based on the recruitment of brilliant talent, doling out of lavish rewards, intolerance of weakness and a demand for total loyalty. For all this Napoleon relied heavily on Louis-Alexandre Berthier, his chief of staff, first marshal and a skilled manager with a remarkable grasp of detail. A short, strong man who was said to be able to fall asleep with one eye open, Berthier was a former topographical engineer who had volunteered to sail across the Atlantic to help the Americans in their war for independence, drawing up a detailed map of the coast of Rhode Island. Berthier divided the army staff into four branches, to cover everything from intelligence to supplies. Divisional commanders had to provide twice-daily updates on the whereabouts of every soldier; these dossiers, like proto- spreadsheets, were sent in 18 large volumes twice a month from the war ministry to Napoleon. In Berthier’s written orders “all names of places, rivers, enemy generals and regiments were underlined,” writes Lord Roberts. New information flowed constantly, from gunpowder stocks or the provision of shoes to the state of troops’ morale. Berthier and Napoleon travelled in a coach fitted with cupboards and drawers for books, maps and a card index listing the location of every unit of the Grande Armée.
