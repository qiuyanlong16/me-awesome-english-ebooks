Once a ward was hit in the middle of the night. Adam ran into the darkness, guided by screams. Then he heard a drone loitering above. Second strikes were common if there was a sign of life, so Adam couldn’t switch on his torch. In the darkness, a young boy clutched his leg and begged him, “Uncle, save me!” But he couldn’t help anyone without light. For hours, the screaming continued, then slowly subsided. Around 70 people died. The hospital was targeted so often that the staff set up operating theatres and emergency rooms in abandoned buildings. But it didn’t make them much safer: the drones just tracked the wounded to these secret outposts, and attacked them too. In spite of the risks, hundreds of people—patients, doctors, staff from other hospitals and their families— moved into Saudi hospital. Nowhere in the city was safe; at least in Saudi they had each other. Room Three, where Adam and his friends lived, was the hospital’s beating heart. It had only four beds, but more than a dozen people would crash there at a time. People didn’t come just to sleep. Room Three was where they went when they were feeling hopeless and alone. Room Three was life. “In Room Three someone was always dancing, someone was always singing,” one surgeon told me later. “Someone was farting.”

Room Three was also directly opposite Room Four, where some of the young female staff slept. Inhabitants of both rooms rolled out mats in the corridor between them, and chatted there over coffee and music. Sometimes they fell in love with each other: there was a spate of marriages between doctors, lab technicians, nurses and pharmacists. The love story that gripped the hospital was that of the two volunteers: Abdullah and Aisha. Abdullah was a great favourite with the hospital staff, as was his mother, Zeinab, who cooked for them. When Aisha’s family home was overrun by RSF fighters in 2024, she moved in with Zeinab. In January 2025 a stray bullet hit Zeinab in the chest. Before she died, she made Abdullah promise to take care of Aisha. Abdullah and Aisha married a few months later. At the ceremony Abdullah asked for a moment’s silence for his mother. Everyone wept. Then they threw a party in one of the damaged emergency rooms. Blue and purple balloons dangled from the walls and people danced to Sudanese love songs. The men threw their hands in the air while women sang along. “It was like there was no war,” Adam said. “We were not living like rats underground.” Two days after Abdullah’s and Aisha’s wedding in April 2025, the RSF overran ZamZam, a camp for families displaced during the last war, which lay on the main road out of el-Fasher. The UN says at least 1,000 people were killed; the real number is probably much higher. The camp had a medical clinic run by Relief International, an aid agency. Though other charities pulled out as the RSF approached, Relief International staff stayed at their posts. One of the doctors, 26-year-old Mahmoud Babikir Idris Adam, also worked shifts at the Saudi hospital. He had been one of the guests at Abdullah’s and Aisha’s wedding celebration. When RSF fighters overran ZamZam they shot Mahmoud dead in his clinic, together with eight of his colleagues. Adam found out shortly afterwards. It was one of the only times in the war that he cried.

After ZamZam, el-Fasher was the RSF’s last significant target in Darfur. RSF soldiers began bulldozing earth into piles to make a barrier round the city. Most exit roads were blocked by RSF checkpoints, and the soldiers manning them were much less inclined to let people pass through for a bribe than they had been in the past. Now travellers were frequently raped, held for ransom or executed. Men of military age were especially at risk. The Yale Humanitarian Lab, which monitored the RSF’s barrier construction from space, described it as a “kill box”. There was hardly anything to eat. By then, the hospital’s windows had all been blown out and replaced by sandbags. The medical staff tried to distract themselves. Adam obtained a PlayStation 5; between shifts he and the other staff lost themselves in “Grand Theft Auto”, “fifa” and “Assassin’s Creed”. The men pooled their remaining supplies of cologne and swapped bottles of Sauvage and Baccarat for novelty. Adam contributed a heady scent called Rose Tobacco. His friend Yasir, who was also a doctor, instituted a weekly fashion ritual called “smart Tuesdays”. He and one of the volunteers would put on their most colourful clothes and take pictures of each other pulling model poses. If they were going to die, they said, they might as well be dressed for it.

There were other small comforts. A black-and-white cat came to the hospital; Yasir adopted it and named it c5 after the incoming RSF rockets. When Bulldoze came home after going missing for three days in August, Adam was so happy he gave the dog some of his precious rice. Music was another balm. A song by Abdullah Alawad, a Sudanese singer with a white streak in his beard, became their anthem. Once Yasir used flour to whiten his own beard, and they recorded a cover version on their phones, bawling out the chorus together: “A person can die but their memory lives on.” No one wanted to acknowledge the fear they all lived with. Wassim, the lab technician, felt suicidal some days. “My family was in danger, everyone around me was in danger, every morning a lot of people died. Children died of starvation,” he said when I met him much later. “I felt like it was better for me to die.” At his lowest point he confided in Adam. The doctor talked him round, promising that God saw their work and would not forget them. That summer, the staff’s resolve started to crumble. The bombardment was unrelenting, and roads out were getting more dangerous every day. Izzadin, one of the surgeons, watched as his operating team dwindled. A few asked him whether they should flee, but he refused to advise them. Some just

stopped showing up; others came tearfully to say goodbye. Izzadin thanked them and gave them his blessing: “May Allah preserve you and help you reach a safe area.” Wassim watched the other lab technicians leave. He was getting calls from a former classmate in the RSF, warning that the militia were about to seize the city and offering to get him out. The classmate spoke with contempt for those still in el-Fasher. He spat out a racist slur sometimes used to describe the dark-skinned people of Darfur: slaves. “He called many times, saying ‘we are coming,’” said Wassim. Eventually, he blocked his old classmate’s number. By the end of the summer, a kilo of rice, which fed a dozen medical staff for a day, cost more than $750. Sometimes surgeons would operate on an injured child and find his bowels full of leaves. Often all the staff had themselves was animal feed made out of crushed peanut waste. They re-used the same coffee grounds for weeks. Some doctors tied sheets around their stomachs to alleviate the stabs of starvation. Adam had weighed 88kg at the beginning of the war: now he was just 38kg. Medical supplies were also scarce, and doctors were forced to use medicines that were several years out of date. The only remaining spinal anaesthesia had expired in 2021. The RSF executed anyone caught smuggling medicine into the city, but some people did it anyway. One of them was an elderly man the Saudi team had operated on; he was frail but would walk three days across the desert to bring back supplies. After hearing the hospital was nearly out of anaesthetic, some women walked to a town 90km away to buy ketamine, which they smuggled back in their bras. The doctors’ cheerfulness became their armour. When a new load of casualties arrived, Yasir would announce, “Life is beautiful!” before getting down to work. But by September it had become hard to maintain. On September 19th a missile slammed into a mosque near the hospital. The imam was married to

Mahasin, the hospital’s administrator, who rushed to the site. Her husband was badly injured and begged her to look for their two young sons in the rubble. By the time she found their bodies, her husband had died. She buried them all in their bedsheets and left the city with her teenage daughter. The hospital was down to two surgeons. Adam and his friends still didn’t want to go. If Saudi went unstaffed, most of the remaining population would leave and the city would be easy to capture. In any case, leaving was now as dangerous as staying. Yasir’s brother was killed at a roadblock trying to escape. A volunteer had left and was never heard from again. By October everything was breaking. A bomb blasted a hole in the wall of the maternity ward that was big enough to walk through. There was an outbreak of cholera. On October 25th Abdullah decided he had to get Aisha, who was now pregnant, to safety. They left with a group of other civilians. Among them was a man who had written social-media posts supporting the Sudanese army. An RSF fighter recognised him at a roadblock, and pulled his family aside. The soldier grabbed the man’s six-year-old son and cut the boy’s throat as he struggled and screamed for his mother. Then he released the man, telling him: “You couldn’t save your son. Go live with this pain.”
