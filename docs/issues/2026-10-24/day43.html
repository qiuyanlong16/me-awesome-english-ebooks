To motivate his marshals, Napoleon issued performance reviews in campaign bulletins, showering praise for heroic achievements along with crushing criticism. Success was rewarded. An attentive manager, Napoleon also liked to offer his marshals personalised advice on how to galvanise the troops. “Do not neglect to fanaticise the soldier and to electrify his imagination to the utmost,” he wrote to André Masséna as he was facing the Austrian army. Yet in the end the management system failed him. As the empire expanded, Napoleon insisted on total control, cramped his marshals’ autonomy and undermined their effectiveness. Worse, he came to weaken the very system he had built. His incentive scheme became capricious, as he overlooked those who deserved praise in some campaign bulletins, or used marshals as scapegoats. Rather than generating creative tension, he fostered destructive rivalry. The danger, Lord Roberts notes, was “when marshals started to see warfare not as a team enterprise so much as a race among rivals for individual triumph and fame”. Napoleon even boasted of stoking “continual jealousy”: “I am careful to keep my ministers and generals at variance among themselves, in order that each may reveal to me the faults of the other.” The marshals began to come to blows after Napoleon’s victory over the combined Russian and Austrian armies at Austerlitz in 1805, and things just got worse. For all his skill, writes Lord Roberts, “Napoleon had a great capacity for sheer spitefulness.” By the time the emperor rode into his last battle in 1815, only two marshals accompanied him. The goal of Kevin Roose, a former reporter for the New York Times, was not to write a grand treatise on what AI means for the world but merely to record this weirdness before it was lost to the vagaries of memory, disappearing message threads and obsolete media-storage formats. Quickly the idea took hold that, given enough data and computing power, transformer-based systems would eventually attain the holy grail: a truly “general” AI, or AGI, matching human smarts and then outstripping them. That doctrine has been the driving force of AI research ever since. And so its history in the West mostly boils down to the interwoven fates of a handful of brilliant, prickly people raising ever more money, panicking each time they get leapfrogged by rivals and splitting off in a blaze of acrimony to start their own firms. In outline, much of the story will be familiar. But Mr Roose captures the madness in a fast-paced style. His account is filled with gossipy anecdotes that highlight just how much the ambitions, ideological divides and personality clashes of a group small enough to fit in a couple of classrooms have shaped what may turn out to be the most consequential technology in modern history. Are there too many art fairs? Probably. But FOMO keeps collectors and dealers going to them Oct 22nd 2026 ART COLLECTORS are like swallows. They flock together, preen and feed (on canapés and champagne). They also migrate seasonally. In October they roost in Paris during Art Basel, which runs from October 21st-25th. (The early birds landed in London for Frieze, another art fair, the previous week.) In December they head to Miami; in March to Maastricht; in May to New York; and in June to the original Basel in Switzerland. And that does not include the more adventurous ones, who zoom off to fairs in Doha, Hong Kong and Seoul. It is as exhausting as it sounds. “Everyone is talking about art-fair fatigue,” says Aurelie Cauchy of the Twentieth, an advisory firm. In 2025 there were 370 major art fairs—more than one for every day of the year. That is up from around 55 in 2000, says Clare McAndrew, an art economist. Can

collectors and dealers maintain their current pace? Beneath the glass roof of the sun-drenched Grand Palais, where Art Basel Paris takes place, there is more gloom than the Instagram posts would have you believe (and not just because of the recent unrest in France). Gallerists are facing what the French call a choix cornélien (difficult choice), says Ms Cauchy. The cost of keeping a physical storefront is growing, but fewer clients are casually popping in. Recently, some have had to lay off staff or close up entirely. Dealers feel pressure to keep travelling to fairs in order to find new clients and keep the artists they represent happy. But this carries an ever-growing price tag, which many struggle to afford. Shipping costs are triple what they were before covid-19, says Thaddaeus Ropac, a prominent gallerist. (It’s not just getting there; all unsold works have to be shipped back.) Gallerists’ own travel costs have travelled higher, too. And the booths themselves have become more expensive to rent and build. Art Basel, for example, charges around $29,000 for its smallest booth and more than $177,000 for the largest. (The square-metre price for large exhibitors is 32% more in real terms than in 2018.) Those fees just get you floor space: exhibitors need to rent carpet, lighting and add other design features. “Fairs are becoming a burden,” especially for smaller and mid- sized galleries, says Valentina Castellani, an art-market expert. Costs have changed the art that appears in fairs. Sculptures are so expensive to ship that they rarely make financial sense. Nor do items with lower price tags, such as many photographs. Since fairs first arrived on the art scene in the 1960s-70s, collectors have found them worthwhile because of their scale and efficiency (so much art is packed into a small space). But art enthusiasts now have fair complaints. One is crowds. Fairs have a lot of guests and such long queues that you’d be forgiven for thinking VIP stands for “very immobile people”. Basel has responded by launching a VVIP preview, Avant-Première, an evening before the VIP opening to offer buyers more quietude. Meanwhile, collectors are culling their calendars and choosing to attend fewer fairs. This month some Americans put the freeze on Frieze and opted to attend only Art Basel Paris instead.

In the future there may be fewer international fairs. People do not want to see the same gallerists everywhere. To lure collectors, organisers will need to offer a more distinctive experience, with more local galleries and artists. This is a broader trend beyond art. “We’ve gone from a moment where everything was meta-globalist to regionalisation,” says Noah Horowitz, the boss of Art Basel. Some gallerists are excited, thinking they may participate in fewer fairs but encounter new local collectors at the ones they do.
