Science & technology | Time for a rethink The second may soon be redefined Several atomic clocks are competing for the job Oct 22nd 2026 IT TAKES ABOUT a second to say “caesium atoms”. And, for nearly 60 years, caesium atoms have said how long a second lasts. That is because caesium-133, an isotope of caesium, switches between two energy states when exposed to microwave radiation, at a rate so regular you could set your watch by it. Indeed, atomic clocks use this steady rhythm to measure time. According to the International System of Units, a second is 9,192,631,770 cycles. But not for much longer. On October 13th the world’s top metrologists convened in Versailles for the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), as they have done roughly once every four years since 1889, to discuss the science of measurement. After three days of debate, they set themselves a deadline: by 2030 they will decide how to redefine the second.

The CGPM oversees the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM), which sets the global standard for the passage of time. It does so by combining measurements from hundreds of atomic clocks based in laboratories around the world, to account for small fluctuations which can cause errors in each individual reading. The result is a single standardised time that co-ordinates everything from financial transactions to satellite navigation. But the BIPM has a problem. To check the accuracy of its standardised time it uses a set of newer clocks, based on different chemical elements, which can measure time more precisely than the best caesium clocks can. Checking the accuracy of the newer clocks requires a definition of the second itself. Using that from a caesium clock is like trying to measure the width of a hair using a ruler whose divisions are wider than the hair. To run the clockwork like clockwork the CGPM needs to untie the second from caesium. Kyle Arnold, a physicist at the National University of Singapore, is part of a team developing a clock based on lutetium ions. While caesium clocks use microwave radiation to trigger transitions between the atom’s energy states, Dr Arnold’s device uses lasers. Such optical clocks “tick” more quickly, allowing time to be measured more finely. Writing in Nature, in September, Dr Arnold’s group reported its lutetium clock had a tick about 1,000 times more regular than the best caesium devices. Atomic clocks must work reliably in different places around the world. Dr Arnold’s device, however, is the size of a room and yet to leave his Singapore lab. Other, more portable, contenders include optical clocks based on ytterbium and strontium atoms suspended by lasers. These come with their own issues. A study published in Physical Review Letters, in July, found that measurements comparing optical clocks based on these elements disagreed with earlier results by more than their known uncertainties could explain. Metrologists must now decide which atomic transition is promoted to the official standard. That requires consensus among yet another team of time lords: the Consultative Committee for Time and Frequency, who are advising the BIPM and CGPM on the second’s fate. With the deadline set for 2030, they have about 100m seconds to reach an agreement. ■

To top things off, the email was signed simply “Wayne”.

Revoking such keys from important central databases is manageable. Checking every system has updated accordingly is hard work. Major web browsers keep their blacklists up-to-date, but many internet-connected systems do not adjust so easily. Some, like many Linux systems, require an active software update; others, like some command-line tools on MacOS, must be manually fixed. Defenders were busily plugging those gaps when, on the morning of October 21st, another problem arrived. A pair of vulnerabilities had been exposed in PHP, a venerable programming language which underpins tens of millions of web applications, including message boards, financial portals and government websites. A patch was promised for the same afternoon. In the meantime, affected organisations were advised by governments to take their systems offline entirely—an instruction not issued lightly. Several government websites in Europe went dark. The advice proved to have been reasonable. Hours after news of the vulnerability broke, and with over 12 hours before a patch was due, its details leaked. The vulnerability was as bad as it gets: a recipe for unauthorised remote code execution (RCE), letting an attacker take control of machines running PHP. Exploiting the vulnerability, as demonstrated by at least one widely shared proof-of-concept, was simple. There is no direct link between the two weaknesses, but one strong relationship: both vulnerabilities seem to have been discovered by AI. The PHP vulnerability was found independently by two members of an AI cyber- defence consortium led by OpenAI, an American firm. The dodgy security keys were “possibly” found using AI too, according to eMudhra, the Indian company which issued them. Certainly, the method of disclosure suggests an unusual approach to research. GitLab, a software development tool, announced a patch for a weakness affecting 12 years of releases that could allow its own RCE, ranking it ten on a ten-point severity scale. Oracle, a tech giant, fixed a similar weakness in Java, its widely used programming language, and ranked the severity 9.1. AI was probably used to uncover these flaws, too.

The episodes illustrate an uncomfortable reality of AI-powered cyber- security: fixing an issue sets a timer for its exploitation. The PHP bug was announced with 18 hours notice before a patch was released, in an effort to prepare defenders to install the update with alacrity. But an accidental release of a video demonstrating the vulnerability allowed others, ai in hand, to reverse-engineer the problem and work out how to exploit it. Shortly after, a security company published its version of the fix six hours early, allowing any attacker to examine exactly what was fixed, and therefore uncover more details of the weakness in unpatched systems.
