How the 2010s remade business The revolutionary decade deserves a revisionist eye Oct 22nd 2026 As decades go, the 2010s were an embarrassment. Tech bosses, now preoccupied with artificial-intelligence models and rockets, talk dismissively of the consumer apps they built back then. Bankers spent it in the drunk tank. After a long summer of executive exuberance, the 2010s were, in many corner offices, a winter of discipline: imposed by governments, the public, activist investors and boards. Yet this decennium horribile has far more to say about business today than the tales from the 1870s, 1920s or 1990s that investors tell themselves. Were the 2010s a cringeworthy decade? Certainly. But a revolutionary one nonetheless. Silicon Valley is ripest for 2010s-revisionism. How is it that America can undertake an enormous, energy-intensive construction boom on a scale never before seen, unbothered by war in the Middle East? Thank the 2010s.

Because of the shale revolution, America ended the decade as a net exporter of energy for the first time in more than half a century. And that endless well of capital now funding the construction of data centres? After the financial crisis, speed limits were imposed on banks and the global lending system was transformed. Many of the capital-markets tricks used today, from special-purpose vehicles to offshore insurance capital, were tried and tested in the 2010s. If this gamble pays off, it will be because AI has revolutionised white-collar work. It is impossible to understand office life without understanding the 2010s. Back then politicians fretted about the gig workers of Uber (2010) or DoorDash (2013). But office life was changing far more. Workers were ordered by the lanyard-Stasi to bring their whole selves to work. When they arrived at the soft-furnished panopticon that is the modern office they built “dashboards” using Datadog (2012) or Snowflake (2014), took Zoom (2013) calls from “hot desks”, perhaps in a WeWork (2010), to the sound of distant machine-gun fire from Slack (2013) messages. When it was happening, the 2010s felt like an age bereft of new business thinking. How wrong that has proven. Effective altruism is a philosophy whose exacting ethical calculus grasps at the universal, but which could have come only from the cloisters of Oxford and Stanford in the early 2010s. The content of its ideas is set out on LessWrong (2009), an online message board, and put into practice by charities like 80,000 Hours (2011). It is now the dominant philosophy of Silicon Valley AI labs, which makes it easily the most influential management idea out there. Politicians likewise find plenty of ideas left on the shelf from the revolutionary decade. Universal basic income was a fringe idea in the equality-obsessed 2010s but is now talked about as serious policy in the AI- obsessed 2020s. The big-is-bad school of antitrust, embraced on both left and right, is a creation of prestigious American law schools during the mid- 2010s. Across the rich world, wealth taxes have become a favourite policy of the left. The central ideas were set out by Thomas Piketty and Gabriel Zucman, a pair of French economists, in the 2010s. 2010s attacks are best repelled by 2010s defences. Anthropic and OpenAI are both structured as public-benefit corporations, a form that, in theory,

makes it easier to balance the interests of society and shareholders. Both labs are incorporated in Delaware—under rules from 2013. In 2019 the Business Roundtable, a powerful CEO-lobby group, announced that a firm’s shareholders would henceforth yield to its stakeholders. Today it reads like an artefact from the high point of corporate high-mindedness. Yet its power endures. This week Anthropic pledged 5% of its shares to non-profit causes. SpaceX is also giving stakes to the public. To misquote Marx, businessmen do not make history as they please, but under conditions passed down to them from the 2010s. Not every big idea from the 2010s survives in full. Central banks’ policy of keeping interest rates around zero ended in 2022. Zealous corporate social- justice campaigns are fewer than before. Still, there are corners of commerce which keep the unreformed 2010s alive. Private-equity funds are devising ways to avoid returning money to their backers, lest they face the post-2010s world. Europe still clings to ESG. So do consultants. McKinsey’s studies of women and ethnic minorities in the workplace were the sacred texts of that era. This month the consultancy demoted a partner for publicly questioning similar ideas. Bain, a rival, recently wrote that “77% of US customers say they are concerned about environmental sustainability, up from 69% last year.” If you say so. Like Japanese soldiers who ignored the end of the second world war, some hardliners from the B-Corp battalion fight on. It is difficult to imagine a wave of nostalgia for the 2010s like that for the 1990s and 2000s. What is there to be nostalgic for? A golden age of gas won’t cut it. A golden age for streaming, maybe. The big beasts of music— Taylor Swift, Drake, etc—have the same ageless commercial quality as the Marvel superhero franchise. But interest in the 2010s will surely grow. The euro zone, which spent much of the 2010s in crisis, now stands on the brink of another. Understanding Silicon Valley means understanding the 2010s. “The Social Reckoning”, a new film about Facebook, is but one of a stream of 2010-gazing works. A series will soon be released about Sam Bankman-Fried, a late-2010s crypto fraudster (interviewed from prison this week by The Economist for our “Money Talks” podcast). Elizabeth Holmes, another 2010s fraudster, also stars in a new film. Then there’s Elon Musk, the world richest man, and now the subject of one of the world’s longest documentaries. His flying car is the

antidote to the 2010s mantra that all we ended up with is 140 characters. Yet Mr Musk is also an electric-car magnate whose power derives from a lawless social network. A man of his age? Finance & economics | The new market-makers AI compute is the new oil—and needs a new market Financial firms race to create instruments to trade processing power Oct 22nd 2026 ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE is changing many things about the modern world. Language is no exception. The word “compute”, for instance, used to be regarded as a verb. Today even grammar pedants grudgingly accept it as a noun, referring to the processing power generated by advanced chips. This new, non-verbal compute is the fuel of a technological revolution. Demand for processing oomph has surged, and it is in short supply. About $360bn was spent on AI compute last year around the world, according to Boston Consulting Group, about 50% more than on copper. By 2030 BCG expects $2.3trn in transactions, roughly the size of the global trade in crude oil today. For all its impressive size, however, the compute market lacks sophistication. The “hyperscalers” (a still somewhat arcane piece of IT jargon for a handful of giant cloud-computing providers including Amazon, Google and Microsoft) and frontier AI labs (such as Anthropic and OpenAI) make deals for tens or even hundreds of billions of dollars of compute. But such arrangements are unavailable to thousands of smaller firms. Buyers and sellers also have few of the derivatives that in established markets allow them to hedge their exposures and speculate. The compute supply chain, then, looks ripe for marketisation. In the most simple form, chipmakers (like Nvidia and AMD) sell advanced AI semiconductors to equipment manufacturers (such as Dell and Hewlett Packard Enterprises), which package the silicon into racks and servers to sell to data-centre operators. Besides the hyperscalers, these include a few large “neoclouds” (one more linguistic innovation, denoting independent

providers like CoreWeave and Nebius) and hundreds of smaller, less well- known firms. The operators, in turn, rent out access to compute to customers for a fixed period, often several years. Customers range from the hyperscalers (which supplement their in-house compute with rentals) to startups running open- source AI models. So far, so simple—in theory. In practice, things quickly get complicated. That is because compute is heterogeneous: different chips are sold in different servers, at different locations, for different time periods. Matching buyers and sellers is finicky. Every business that needs processing power has a “compute guy” (or several) buying second-hand capacity through an opaque network of middlemen. Many of these informal brokers, some shadier than others, tout their offerings through messaging apps like WhatsApp and Telegram. The whole thing looks less like trading a high-tech commodity and more like peddling illegal drugs. In a market that is so fractured and immature, providers and purchasers alike prefer the safety of long-term contracts. On paper, these guarantee capacity to users and revenues for themselves. In reality, given how fast the technology and the market of AI are evolving, it is hard to tell how much such deals are worth: today’s buyers and sellers may not be around in a few years to honour their commitments. The surging yields on CoreWeave’s bonds highlight the uncertainty. Who will make a killing? And who may not make it at all? With lenders struggling to price risk, many smaller operators buying and renting compute face double-digit interest rates. Fortunately for everyone involved, proper markets are being built around the compute supply chain. Some budding bourses, such as Compute Exchange, list GPUs (the increasingly familiar shorthand for graphics-processing units, AI’s silicon workhorse) by location, rental duration, price and availability. Titans of global derivatives trading, including CME Group and the Intercontinental Exchange, hope to launch their own compute futures. They have teamed up with startups pioneering compute indices, pegged to the rental price of popular AI chips. They await regulatory approval.
