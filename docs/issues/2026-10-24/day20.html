The chaos was the result of a combustible mix on the streets—and an inadequate police presence. Laurent Nuñez, the interior minister, conceded that the police had “lost control”, and a parliamentary inquiry has been opened. Those on the streets fell into three groups. The first were the half- million students, teachers and unionists who protested peacefully against underinvestment in education and the cost of living. “The casseurs are discrediting our movement,” complained Pauline, a 20-year-old university student, who said she was demonstrating against “everything”, from overcrowded lecture halls and high petrol prices to police repression. The unpopular president was also on her list. “Macron, t’es mort” (Macron, you’re dead) read graffiti near a smashed-up bus shelter, next to “ACAB”, or “all cops are bastards”. A second group, the casseurs, have a history of infiltrating demos. Dressed in black and full-hooded, often wearing ski goggles and face masks, they looked to pick a fight. In France there are 600-800 members of such ultra- left groups, often anti-capitalist anarchists, said a parliamentary report in 2023. They are agile, covert and deft at evading the police. Of 48 “black blocs” (a group of casseurs) arrested after protests in 2023, 85% were men and 75% aged under 33. Most are well educated and not poor, noted the report. They seek “to create trouble in order to overturn the system”, said Mr Nuñez, then head of the Paris police. The third group were opportunistic looters. Messages shared on TikTok guided youngsters to smashed-up shopfronts. In Bordeaux one young man casually swung stolen goods inside a shopping basket; others ripped looted Nike trainers from their boxes and ran. Within hours stolen goods were put on sale online—“jumper stolen during the demos” read one description— before the websites took them down. Some 3,000 shops and businesses were looted or vandalised.

Intelligence services had forecast a turnout of 200,000; the number was over twice that. Some 60,000 police officers were deployed, about a third less than during the worst days of the gilets jaunes protests. During earlier protests police were accused of brutality. This time shopkeepers were enraged that they were left to fend for themselves. Calm returned after Sébastien Lecornu, the prime minister, authorised partial curfews in Bordeaux, Paris, Marseille and other cities. Soldiers will help keep guard for the next protests, planned for October 24th. The underlying tension is linked to the election, which is polarising politics. After nearly a decade in office, the centrist Mr Macron is constitutionally barred from running again. Many protesting students have no memory of life under a different leader. France wants change, but is deeply divided about what should come next. Supporters of Mr Mélenchon, who joined the demo in Paris, saw the protests as a show of strength for their champion. Beforehand 69% of the French told a poll they backed the protesters, rising to 92% among his supporters. Backing for his Unsubmissive France (LFI) party is strong among the young and in the ethnically diverse banlieues, or suburbs, where schools are in the worst shape. A poll taken after the riots showed his base had held strong. Mr Lecornu, who blames the LFI for “exploiting” the protests, warned that the extreme left is using the violence as a “warm-up round” for the election. Mr Mélenchon blames the government for the disorder. The real beneficiary, though, is Ms Le Pen. She got a small bump in the polls, which now give her 34-38% in the first round of the presidential election; Mr Mélenchon is on 15-20%. On October 18th she gave a presidential-style address, blaming the violence on “politicised casseurs” and “criminals from the banlieue”. Ms Le Pen regards the protests as evidence that Mr Mélenchon is unfit for office and that Mr Macron cannot control the country. One poll finds she would win a run-off against Mr Mélenchon by 68% to 32%. Debate is shifting to law and order, which plays into her hands. Donald Trump claimed that France is being “burnt to the ground” by migrants. But nearly all of the demonstrators are French, with white people as well represented as any other ethnicity. A government minister hinted at a racial

element, but just among looters, blaming “a criminal fraction from the banlieue”. This is fertile ground for foreign interference too. NewsGuard, an American disinformation monitor, found that 14 fake reports, often pointing the finger at migrants, were linked to a Russian influence operation. An AI-generated image of the Palestinian flag atop the Eiffel Tower was viewed 3.7m times. Mr Macron said there was indeed “foreign manipulation”, not of the events themselves, but about the narrative of what happened. The violence risks distracting France from the public debate it needs: about what sort of welfare system it can afford and how to generate the wealth required to sustain it. The public finances are in a dire state and the bond markets are jittery. Yet the centre is split, and lacks a compelling candidate. And the leading politicians vow to lower the retirement age, thus depriving themselves of future savings on the single biggest item of public spending. Part of France understands this. The silent constituency was out again in the terrace cafés the day after the riots; a waiter in Bordeaux called the violence “scandalous”. But radicals are masterful at drowning out such voices. Unruly protests have shaken Albania, Italy and beyond Radicals are turning demos into riots—and usually hurting their causes Oct 22nd 2026 In several European countries, leftists inspired by weeks of protests in France have launched mass demonstrations of their own. But as in France, such protests are likely to benefit the right if they turn violent. The clearest case is Italy, where in October secondary-school students began imitating their French peers and occupying classrooms in protest against underfunding. Those have grown into broad demos over the cost of living, backed by left-wing parties and unions. Things changed on October 17th, when several marches were hijacked by black-clad gangs. Riots erupted in Turin and Bologna, and fires were set in Milan’s Piazza del Duomo. Shops were looted, dozens of police injured and hundreds arrested. An October 17th Movement has emerged on Telegram

and Instagram, calling for protests every Saturday until the government falls. A march is planned in Rome on October 24th, and many expect violence. The rioters lack obvious leaders but have an unofficial soundtrack: Fabri Fibra’s “In Italia 2024”, a doom-laden anthem of social exclusion and rebellion. “We’re only at the beginning,” says Marracash, a rapper from Barona, a poor neighbourhood in Milan. The immigrant backgrounds of many rioters have provided ammunition for Matteo Salvini, head of the populist-right Lega party, to call for barring the Italian-born children of immigrants from acquiring citizenship if convicted of crimes. Italy will hold a general election next year. Giorgia Meloni, the populist- right prime minister, has challenged the progressive Democratic Party and Five Star Movement to condemn the violence. By backing the legitimate demonstrations, they risk seeming to condone the rioting. Fear of disorder will probably benefit the right, says Raffaele Marchetti of the Luiss University in Rome. On October 20th Ms Meloni’s cabinet allowed preventive detention of minors, and gave policing powers to 500 soldiers in Milan. Yet the riots may benefit not her, but her rivals on the right. Roberto Vannacci, a far-right former general, and his opposition National Future party have said the police should use rubber bullets and dogs to disperse protesters. Demos elsewhere also pit left against right. Spaniards have been protesting over housing costs since September, backed by Pedro Sánchez, the Socialist prime minister. Big protests over the cost of living have broken out in Portugal and Greece. On October 17th a few hundred far-left activists clashed with police in Brussels. But the most violent demonstrations outside France and Italy are in Albania, and have a different background. Albanians have been protesting against the government of Edi Rama, the prime minister, since May 31st. For months the demonstrations were peaceful; prompted by a plan by Jared Kushner, Donald Trump’s son-in-law, to build a beach resort in a nature reserve, they were dubbed the Flamingo Revolution. But since October 13th a violent wing of the movement has attacked ministries, courts, banks and shops.

The Flamingo protests were always about more than resort development. Despite economic progress in recent years, Albania is one of the poorest countries in Europe and corruption levels are high. Mr Rama claims the riots are encouraged by Russia and Iran. He refuses to resign, and promises huge bonuses to police to take back the streets. One academic, who requested anonymity for fear of retaliation, said the protesters’ belief that they could force the prime minister out had been misplaced, but that his hard-line response had destroyed his legitimacy: “I don’t think there is any coming back from this.” Most protesters despise the opposition leader, who was himself driven out as president by protests in 1997. But this week’s riots could mark the beginning of Mr Rama’s end. The riots in Albania, France, Italy and elsewhere do have one thing in common.
