Ignore the doomsayers. American democracy is in good shape A former ally asks why Donald Trump abandoned his own legacy

Ignore the doomsayers. American democracy is in good shape Its resilience rests not on politicians’ restraint but on the robustness of institutional arrangements, writes Frances Lee Oct 22nd 2026 AMERICA’S TWO main parties do not agree on much, but they share a slogan. “We’ve got to be too big to rig,” Kamala Harris told an audience recently. Days later Donald Trump called for a midterm win “too big to rig” because “they rig elections in this country.” Both parties urge their own supporters to vote while warning that the other side cheats. Scholars and journalists, meanwhile, have settled on a diagnosis: democratic backsliding. Many voters themselves are anxious. Looking towards the midterms on November 3rd, only 49% of respondents expect a free and fair election. Under Mr Trump’s influence Republicans are especially cynical. Democrats worry about election interference from his administration. Minority voters

fear harassment from Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents at polling stations. Anxieties notwithstanding, American elections are healthier than the backsliding narrative allows. Indeed, there was also considerable anxiety in advance of both the 2022 and 2024 elections. Then, as now, Americans worried about election interference, voter fraud and political violence. But in the end turnout was high by historical standards and the vast majority of Americans expressed confidence in the outcomes. American democracy rests on durable institutions capable of checking concentrated power. Midterm elections are important in this regard. The “midterm law”—that the president’s party loses seats midway through his term—has held in 20 of the 22 votes since 1938. Mr Trump’s first term followed the pattern: Republicans lost their majority in the House of Representatives in 2018. Notably, the president’s party loses seats in midterms even when most Americans approve of the president’s performance. The penalty grows when the president is unpopular and the economy weak. Mr Trump’s approval rating languishes in the mid-30s. In September consumer confidence fell to its lowest level since 2014, partly owing to high fuel prices. Across midterms since 1992 the president’s party has lost an average of 25 House seats. The current Republican majority disappears with a net loss of three. In an effort to forestall losses, Mr Trump induced Republican-controlled states to redraw their electoral maps. The resulting gerrymanders probably netted some Republican seats, albeit fewer than they had hoped. But here again American institutions retain safeguards against a president’s effort at electoral deck-stacking. A president can demand new maps only in states where his party holds the pen, and Democrats in California answered a gerrymander in Texas with one of their own. The Cook Political Report projects a net Republican gain of four seats from redistricting, not nearly enough to protect the party’s House majority. Mr Trump floated and later disavowed the idea of postponing or cancelling the midterms. Regardless, that scenario was always wildly implausible. Election day is fixed by federal statute, and the constitution sets the date

when office-holders’ terms end. The decentralised administration of elections across thousands of local jurisdictions denies the president any easy lever to manipulate outcomes. Mr Trump’s attempt to turn the postal service into such a lever against mail-in ballots was thwarted by the courts. More likely than interference with the election is that unsuccessful office- seekers will refuse to concede, claiming their opponents cheated. Nothing is more basic to democratic legitimacy than the loser’s consent. Many feared that Mr Trump’s refusal to concede in 2020 would spread across the political system. It has not. Bright Line Watch, a research group, examined all 884 congressional races in 2020 and 2022. The overwhelming majority of losing candidates publicly acknowledged defeat. Explicit refusals were concentrated among first-time candidates who had lost by wide margins. And there were fewer such refusals in 2022 than in 2020. Candidates with political careers to protect still concede. When they do, complaints of election “rigging” lose their force. None of this is cause for complacency. My research with Geoffrey Layman and Christina Wolbrecht finds that party activists, long regarded as guardians of democratic norms, are now no more committed to losers’ consent than ordinary citizens. Any deterioration in basic democratic values is worrying. Nevertheless, the record of American elections since 2020 has been encouraging. Election administration has improved. Although many career administrators resigned amid increased harassment after 2020, the voters’ experience actually got better. In both 2022 and 2024 the share of people waiting more than 30 minutes to cast their ballot was lower than in 2020, and the long-documented racial gap narrowed: by 2024 black, white, Hispanic and Asian voters reported comparable waiting times. Overall, the evidence points to a resilient democracy. That resilience rests not on politicians’ restraint but on institutional arrangements that are difficult to manipulate: fixed election dates, decentralised administration and officials independently elected in their own constituencies, with their own interests to protect.
